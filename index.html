Why local matters

A Bridge hedge is not a generic hedge.

Bridge sits in the Nailbourne valley on the old Roman Watling Street, the London to Dover road, with the North Downs rising either side. The Nailbourne itself is a winterbourne, a chalk stream that runs strongly in wet years and dries to nothing most summers, and the cold air that pools in the valley bottom on a still winter night means genuine frost pockets, not a coastal problem, a proper inland one. Up on the downland above the village the soil is thin, chalky and free-draining; down by the stream it holds more moisture and traps the cold.

I work to that reality, not against it. Yew, hawthorn and beech on the chalk slopes where they have grown for centuries. Hornbeam and field maple on the damper valley-bottom boundaries. A careful eye on anything tender that was planted for a look rather than for this ground, it is usually the first thing to suffer in a hard Kentish frost. And a tape measure on any stem approaching the 75mm conservation-area threshold before I touch it near Bifrons Park.

Inside the conservation area?

I handle the paperwork.

The Bifrons Park Conservation Area sits within Bridge parish, adjacent to the recreation ground and within the wider Kent Downs Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty. Any tree with a stem over 75mm at chest height inside the CA needs six weeks' written notice to Canterbury City Council before pruning. I check the boundary and file the section-211 notice on your behalf, included in every quote.
