Sharpening hedge shears: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

A blunt pair of hand shears does not cut hedge growth, it tears it, and torn cuts are rougher to look at and more vulnerable to disease entry than a clean edge. Paul Sellers is a British master craftsman based in Oxfordshire who has taught hand-tool technique to hundreds of thousands of people through his YouTube channel. His guide to sharpening garden shears covers the same principles that apply to forged steel hedge shears. This article takes that demonstration and adds what it cannot include: the specific reasons why regular sharpening matters more around Bridge than most people expect, from the resin that yew hedges leave on blades in summer to the damp conditions on the Nailbourne valley floor that accelerate rust between seasons.

Watch first: Sharpening Garden Shears (Paul Sellers)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=KD8BVZ15C6E  ·  Channel: Paul Sellers

What the video shows

Paul Sellers approaches sharpening as a craftsman rather than a gardener, which is precisely why the technique transfers so well. Unlike gardening videos that describe the angle loosely and move on, Paul explains the underlying geometry: why you sharpen only the bevel face, never the flat back, and why keeping that distinction consistent is what produces an edge that lasts. The video demonstrates:

  • The bevel face only. Garden shears, like bench chisels, have a single bevel ground on the outer face of each blade. Sharpening the bevel restores the cutting angle; grinding the flat back destroys it. Paul is clear about which face receives the stone.
  • Flat stone technique. He uses a flat diamond stone (or oilstone) held firmly on a bench surface, working the bevel across it with slow, deliberate strokes at a consistent angle. The same technique works equally well on hedge shears: the bevel angle is shallower than a chisel but the method is identical.
  • The wire edge (burr). Sharpening the bevel pushes a fine wire of metal onto the flat back. Paul shows how to remove it with a single light stroke on the flat face, then a strop on leather or dense cardboard. Leaving the wire edge in place will cause it to fold over and dull the edge on the first few cuts.
  • Testing the finished edge. The paper-slicing test: a sharp blade should cut cleanly through a sheet of paper held freehand. Tearing rather than cutting means the edge needs another pass on the stone.
  • Both blades, same geometry. Shears work as scissors. One sharp blade and one blunt blade will still tear rather than cut, because the blunt edge has to be dragged through the stem. Both blades need the same treatment.

Paul's video was made for general garden shears rather than hedge shears specifically, but the geometry is identical and the technique applies without modification. The main practical difference is scale: hedge shears have longer blades, so each sharpening pass covers more surface, but the angle, the stone motion and the burr-removal step are the same.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Yew resin. The North Downs chalk around Bridge and along the lanes to Patrixbourne and Lower Hardres carries a high density of yew hedging (Taxus baccata). Yew produces a sticky resin when cut, particularly on warm days in late summer, that transfers to blade faces and then picks up fine dust and plant debris. Left to harden, it forms a gummy residue that dulls the cutting edge and increases friction through the cut. Before any sharpening makes sense, this residue needs to come off: a rag dampened with WD-40 or a purpose-made blade oil will dissolve it. I do this before the sharpening step on every pair of shears that has been used on yew. The yew hedge pruning guide covers the annual cut timing and technique in detail.

Chalk mineral deposits and hard water. Bridge sits on the North Downs chalk aquifer, and the tap water in the area is hard, with high mineral content. Box (Buxus) hedging releases a milky sap when cut; combined with the mineral-rich water used to rinse tools, this can leave a calcite and residue crust on blade faces. The crust is not damaging in itself, but it fills the surface finish and makes it harder to assess the condition of the metal. White spirit on a cloth removes most of it before sharpening. This is not a consideration that would come up in a video filmed in less chalky ground.

Nailbourne valley humidity and rust. The Nailbourne is a chalk winterbourne, a stream that runs when the water table rises, and the valley floor near Bishopsbourne and Barham stays noticeably damper than the chalk slopes above. Tools left without a protective coat through the wetter months pick up surface rust faster on the valley floor than they would stored in a drier chalk-slope setting. Wiping blades with a thin coat of camellia oil or general tool oil before putting shears away at the end of the season takes a minute and prevents the light surface rust that can make a freshly sharpened edge feel rough on the first cut of spring. It also prevents the pivot bolt from seizing.

Sharpening before the nesting season closes, not after. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. The active nesting period runs from 1 March through to the end of August, with some late broods extending into September. The last week of August is the point at which I want hand shears in their best condition: the formal box and yew edges that benefit most from hand-shear work are the ones being finished off before the end of the season window, and a sharp edge means the work is done faster and more cleanly than a blunt one dragged through dense growth. I sharpen before the season starts and touch up after every two or three jobs. The nesting-law and timing guide covers the full legal position and the nest-check steps.

Hand shears and power trimmers: when each is the right tool. Power hedge trimmers are the right choice for the bulk of maintenance work on established hedges around Bridge. Hand shears have a different role: fine topiary cuts on box balls and yew specimens, tight inside corners where a powered blade will not reach cleanly, and any situation where vibration or noise matters. Within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, which covers much of the older parkland and estate boundary planting near Patrixbourne Road, hand-shear work on formal hedge features gives a cleaner surface finish on dense yew and box and avoids the bruising that a heavy reciprocating blade can leave. The conservation-area notice requirements under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 govern structural works and significant reductions rather than routine maintenance; the choice of hand shear versus powered trimmer is a quality and finish question for routine trimming, not a legal one. I survey the stems before booking any job near the CA where significant work is being considered.

Pivot adjustment. Paul Sellers' video covers the blades, which is the right focus. The other area where hand shears most commonly fail is the pivot point. A loose pivot causes the blades to scissor past each other rather than meeting cleanly, which produces a tearing action regardless of how sharp the edges are. The pivot bolt on most quality forged hedge shears, including UK and European makers such as Burgon and Ball and Felco, can be tightened with a standard spanner or hex key. A drop of oil on the pivot between uses prevents the stiffness that develops in damp storage conditions and leads to over-forcing the cut.
