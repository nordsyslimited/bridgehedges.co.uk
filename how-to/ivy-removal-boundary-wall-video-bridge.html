Removing ivy from garden walls and boundaries: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Ivy arrives on old garden walls without being planted, carried in by birds, and within a decade it can cover a significant run of flint or brick boundary. Whether to remove it, and how to do it safely for the wall and within the wildlife law, is not straightforward. Burncoose Nurseries, the specialist Cornish plant nursery with a strong RHS show record, made a short demonstration video titled "Removing ivy" that covers the core technique. This guide works through what it demonstrates, then adds what it cannot: the bat roost law, the nesting-season window, and the particular care needed with the old lime-mortar walls that run through Bridge, Patrixbourne and Bishopsbourne.

Watch first: Removing ivy (Burncoose Nurseries)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=iVj8pK_M680  ·  Channel: Burncoose Nurseries

What the video shows

Burncoose Nurseries is a long-established specialist plant nursery and mail-order business in Gwennap, Cornwall, with a significant RHS Gold Medal record at Chelsea and Hampton Court. Their garden tips video series covers practical plant and garden management with a direct, hands-on approach. The ivy removal demonstration covers the main decision points and practical steps:

  • Why cut before you strip. The video makes clear that pulling live ivy straight off a wall is the wrong approach. Live ivy clings via aerial rootlets that lock into mortar and brick, and tearing it off while it is still alive pulls the surface with it, damaging pointing and sometimes dislodging mortar entirely. The right sequence is to sever all the main stems at the base first, then wait for the growth above to die back and dry out before attempting to strip it off.
  • The stem-cutting step. The demonstration shows cutting all ivy stems as close to the ground as possible, using loppers for younger growth and a pruning saw where stems have thickened into old woody material. Established ivy trunks on walls that have been left for many years can be surprisingly substantial: a mature stem base is more like a small tree trunk than a garden shrub, and loppers will not cut it cleanly. A sharp pruning saw is the right tool, working close to ground level.
  • Protective equipment. The video notes the importance of gloves throughout, since ivy sap contains falcarinol and other compounds that cause contact dermatitis in some people. The reaction can be delayed by several hours, which means people sometimes strip ivy bare-handed without immediate effect and do not connect the rash that follows to the job. Gloves and long sleeves are sensible for any substantial ivy clearance.
  • Clearing the dried growth. Once the severed growth has died and dried, the video shows the approach to stripping it back: working gently, taking manageable sections rather than trying to pull large masses, and working from the top of the section downward where possible so dead material falls clear rather than catching behind what is still attached. For large areas, this is a significant amount of material to handle and dispose of.
  • Regrowth from the stump. The video addresses the near-certainty that cut stumps will re-shoot. Ivy is a persistent plant and the crown at ground level will push new growth for several seasons after cutting. The demonstration makes clear that one visit rarely ends the problem: follow-up removal of new shoots is needed for two or three growing seasons until the root system exhausts itself.

The video does not address the nesting-season law, bat roost risk, old lime-mortar wall assessment, or the conservation-area rules that apply to some boundaries in Bridge and the Nailbourne valley. Those are the parts worth reading on.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Nesting season: the most significant timing constraint. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest of any wild bird. Ivy is one of the most important nesting habitats in a British garden. Its dense, year-round evergreen structure, retained close to walls and fences, is exactly what wrens, blackbirds, robins, song thrushes and dunnocks choose for nest sites. Ivy growing on a sunny south-facing wall can hold nests from early March through to late August. The practical implication is that ivy clearance has a narrow safe window: October to the end of February. Within that window, nesting is at a minimum and any bird activity in the ivy is likely to be roosting rather than nesting. I do a visual inspection before every ivy clearance job regardless of the time of year, looking for occupied nests and for the structural signs of a nest (a cup of woven plant material, dried grass, mud lining) even in an apparently dormant structure. Finding an occupied nest stops the work. Outside the October to February window, I will not start an ivy clearance job without first carrying out a thorough nest check, and I advise against booking ivy clearance for the main growing season without accepting that the job may have to be deferred. The nesting-law timing guide explains what a proper pre-work nest check involves and what records are worth keeping.

Bat roost risk. All UK bat species and their roosts are protected under Schedule 5 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 and by the Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017. It is an offence to deliberately capture, injure or kill a bat, to deliberately disturb a bat, or to damage or destroy a bat roost, whether the roost is in use or not (bat roosts retain their protection even when bats are not present, since bats return to the same roosts year after year). Common pipistrelle and soprano pipistrelle, the two bat species most frequently encountered in gardens in this part of Kent, regularly roost in ivy growing on walls and in the dense base of old, established ivy. The ivy provides insulation, structural support and concealment. Established ivy on a south-facing wall is a particularly productive roost, holding warmth even in cool weather. If I am called to ivy that has been in place for ten or more years on a wall with no previous disturbance, I expect to find bat sign: droppings (small, dark, dry pellets) in the ivy base or at the base of the wall directly below the thickest growth. If droppings or bats are found, the job stops and Natural England must be contacted for a mitigation licence before any further work. This is not optional. The penalty for disturbing or destroying a bat roost can be a substantial fine. The correct window to avoid bat disturbance is roughly November to March, when bats are in hibernation and are least likely to be present in an external roost. Even in that window, a formal bat check on old, dense, undisturbed ivy is advisable before starting work. If you are unsure, engage a licensed bat surveyor.

Old lime-mortar walls: assess before you strip. Many of the garden boundary walls in Bridge, Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne and Bishopsbourne are old flint-and-chalk constructions with lime mortar. Some date to the eighteenth or nineteenth century and have never been repointed. The mortar on these walls is often in variable condition: sound in sections, friable and crumbling in others, particularly at the top of the wall where frost and water damage accumulate. Ivy growing on a sound, well-pointed wall does relatively little mechanical damage: the aerial rootlets (holdfasts) attach to the surface of the mortar and stone rather than growing through it, and established ivy can even protect an old wall by moderating temperature fluctuations and shedding water from the face. On a wall with deteriorating mortar, the picture is different: rootlets penetrate into existing cracks and voids, and the weight of large masses of old ivy can pull pointing out of joints that are already failing. The critical issue after removal is that stripping ivy off an old wall exposes the face to frost and weather for the first time in decades. Protected, slightly damp mortar will now freeze and thaw repeatedly, and previously stable areas can begin to fail rapidly. I always assess the mortar condition at a sample of points before an ivy clearance job on an old wall, and I advise having the pointing inspected and repaired in weak areas before or immediately after stripping. Leaving a large section of old lime-mortar wall bare through a Kent winter without attention to the pointing can cause more damage than the ivy did.

Ivy's genuine ecological value. Before committing to full ivy removal, it is worth being clear about what is being lost. Hedera helix is one of a small number of native plants that provides significant wildlife value across all four seasons: nectar and pollen for ivy bees, hoverflies and late-season butterflies in October and November; berries for blackbirds, redwings and fieldfares through the winter; dense year-round shelter for wrens, robins and hedgehogs; and the bat roost habitat noted above. Full clearance of a large ivy-covered wall removes all of that in one visit. For many jobs I am asked to do, the right answer is managed reduction rather than full removal: cutting the main front growth back hard to a retained framework every two or three years, keeping the ivy from reaching the gutters and windows, while maintaining a residual canopy of perhaps 30 to 40 centimetres against the wall. This keeps the wildlife value, keeps the wall covered and protected, and avoids the bat and nesting risks associated with full clearance. The hedge clearance guide covers how to think about the ecology-versus-management balance when dealing with established plants on a boundary.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area and boundary structures. The Bifrons Park Conservation Area covers parts of Bridge and the land around it, and includes sections of old boundary wall that are part of the historic character of the area. Canterbury City Council has a duty to preserve and enhance the character of the CA. Work that damages a historic boundary wall, even incidentally during ivy removal, can have planning implications. For walls that are listed in their own right, listed building consent may be required before any work that affects the fabric of the structure. If you are uncertain whether a wall is listed or falls within the CA boundary, check with Canterbury City Council before starting. I do this as a matter of course for older walls in Bridge and the lane villages. The conservation-area guide covers the CA notification process and how to establish whether a structure has listed status.
