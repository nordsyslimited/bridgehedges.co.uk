Sympathetic hedge cutting for wildlife: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

In 2025 the UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology (UKCEH) and the National Association of Agricultural Contractors (NAAC) produced a short practical film on sympathetic hedge cutting, presented by Chris Gardner, a working hedge contractor and NAAC vice-chair. It came out of a UKCEH survey of contractors, farmers and stakeholders that found more accessible, ground-level guidance was the single most useful thing for improving hedge condition on working farms. This guide pairs the film with what it cannot cover: the specific regulatory framework that applies to field boundary hedges in England since 2024, the species mix and growth rates of the North Downs chalk hedges and Nailbourne valley floor hedgerows around Bridge, and the conservation area considerations that apply in this parish.

Watch first: Sympathetic hedge cutting – practical tips and insights! (UKCEH)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=-wtGsvYybjc  ·  Produced by: UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology (UKCEH)

What the video shows

The film is six minutes long and is aimed at professional agricultural contractors and farm managers. Chris Gardner presents the content from the perspective of a working hedge contractor rather than a researcher or conservationist, which makes it practically grounded. The core argument is that sympathetic cutting does not require a contractor to do less work or accept a less tidy result. It requires a different approach to what the job looks like, particularly on the timing and profile decisions that are made before the machine moves. The film covers:

  • Why the guidance was developed. The UKCEH survey found that many contractors and farm managers wanted clearer, more practical guidance, not more ecological theory. The film is a direct response to that: a practitioner speaking to other practitioners about technique, not a conservation body explaining why hedgerows matter.
  • The benefit to the farmer and the contractor, not only the environment. Gardner makes the point that well-managed hedgerows with good structure and berry crops are more valued by landowners and are more likely to result in work being rebooked. Sympathetic management creates a better hedge, which is a better product to offer as a contractor. The film is explicit that this is not in tension with running a commercial agricultural contracting business.
  • What sympathetic cutting looks like on the ground. The focus is on the decisions made during the job: not always cutting to the same line, allowing the profile to extend gradually rather than holding it to the same face year after year, and leaving sections or areas where the natural habit of the hedge is allowed to express itself without producing an unmanageable boundary problem. The film presents these as practical choices available to any contractor on any job, not special interventions that require extra time or equipment.
  • Timing relative to the wildlife value of the hedge. Gardner addresses the timing question from the contractor's perspective: when a hedge is cut relative to the berry crop, the blossom cycle and the nesting season makes a significant difference to its wildlife value and to how it recovers in subsequent years. The film situates this within the practical reality of a contractor's scheduling rather than presenting it as a fixed ecological rule the contractor must work around.

The film does not address the specific regulatory framework that now applies to field boundary hedges in England, the difference between agricultural hedgerows and garden hedges in terms of legal coverage, or the species and soil conditions particular to any specific area. Those are the parts worth reading further for work around Bridge.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The Management of Hedgerows (England) Regulations 2024 changed the legal position for field boundary work. Since 23 May 2024, the Management of Hedgerows (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/680) have prohibited cutting, trimming, uprooting or otherwise destroying any hedgerow on or adjacent to agricultural land in England between 1 March and 31 August. This reinstates protections that had lapsed, and applies to any contractor or landowner working on a field boundary hedge anywhere in the Nailbourne valley, on the chalk slopes around Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne, Bishopsbourne, Kingston and Barham, or on the downland hedges running up towards Lower Hardres. For the full detail on how both this regulation and the nesting law work in practice, the healthy hedgerow management guide covers both frameworks and the two-to-three year cut cycle that PTES research supports. The cut window for agricultural field boundary hedges is now fixed at 1 September through to the end of February, and I do all field boundary hedge work in this area inside that window.

Agricultural hedgerows and garden hedges are covered by different rules. The 2024 Regulations apply only to hedgerows on or adjacent to agricultural land. A garden boundary hedge in Bridge village, even one adjacent to a field, may not fall under the Regulations depending on how the land is classified. What it will always fall under is section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird, throughout the nesting season from 1 March to 31 August. In practice, both frameworks point to the same window: I do not cut any hedge, garden or agricultural, between 1 March and 31 August without a nest survey. The nesting law timing guide covers the survey steps and the legal position for garden hedges in full.

The North Downs chalk hedges and the Nailbourne valley floor behave differently. The field boundary hedgerows on the chalk slopes above Bridge, running towards Patrixbourne and Lower Hardres, are dominated by hawthorn and blackthorn with elder, dog rose, field maple and wayfaring tree in the mix. These hedges are on free-draining alkaline ground and tend to put on moderate growth. A sympathetic approach here means letting the cut line creep outward gradually and leaving sections at corners and gateways uncut to maintain a rough fringe, without the hedge running out of the boundary. On the heavier, moister valley floor of the Nailbourne, particularly around Bishopsbourne and Bekesbourne, elder and dog rose grow notably faster. Valley-floor hedgerows managed sympathetically will put on more growth per cycle than their chalk-slope equivalents. The sympathetic approach is still right; it just requires the incremental line to be planned with enough room for a hedge that is genuinely vigorous. The hawthorn field boundary trimming guide covers the chalk-versus-valley-floor distinction for the most common North Downs hedgerow species in more detail.

Badger sets and the law before any mechanical hedge cutting. On rural field boundary hedges, particularly those that have not been cut for two or more years, a check for active badger setts is a legal requirement before bringing in a flail or hedge trimmer. Under the Protection of Badgers Act 1992, it is an offence to damage or obstruct a badger sett. Active setts are not uncommon in the established hedgerow banks and ditchlines of the Nailbourne valley. I check any rural boundary I have not visited recently before starting mechanical work, and I will postpone a job rather than risk a sett that is not clearly inactive. This is not covered in the UKCEH film but it is part of what sympathetic field boundary work looks like in practice on this ground.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area. Any hedgerow stems within or adjoining the Bifrons Park Conservation Area that have been allowed to grow into standard trees can reach the 75mm diameter at 1.5m threshold that triggers a six-week prior-notification requirement to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Routine sympathetic trimming of an established field boundary hedge generally does not reach this threshold, but structural work on hedgerows that contain mature stems does. I check the address against the CA boundary before any structural work of that kind. The conservation area tree and hedge notice guide covers the notification process for Bridge.
