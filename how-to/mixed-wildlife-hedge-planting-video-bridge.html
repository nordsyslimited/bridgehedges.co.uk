Planting a mixed wildlife hedge (cell-grown plants): a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Heathwood Nurseries are a UK specialist in native hedging plants, and their short planting guide is one of the clearest demonstrations of the cell-grown method you will find. Cell-grown plants are the alternative to bare-root stock: they come with an intact root ball in a plug or cell, they can go in the ground at a wider range of times than bare-root, and they are useful when you need a hedge outside the core November to March dormancy window. What Wayne at Heathwood cannot cover in a three-minute nursery guide is which species to choose for the particular split between chalk slopes and the Nailbourne valley floor around Bridge, and what makes a native hedge worth planting for wildlife rather than just for boundary screening.

Watch first: How to plant a Mixed Native Hedge – Heathwood Nurseries

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=oN_D3v9YQ8s

What the video shows

Wayne from Heathwood Nurseries works through the full process for planting a cell-grown mixed native hedge, covering the differences from bare-root planting and the practical steps that matter most for establishment. The main points are:

  • The advantage of cell-grown stock: each plant comes in a plug with an intact root system, so there is no dormancy requirement. Cell-grown plants can go in from late summer through to spring, broadening the planting window beyond the bare-root season.
  • Spacing guidance for a native mixed hedge: a staggered double row, with plants set roughly 30 to 45 centimetres apart within each row and the rows offset from each other, gives the density that closes gaps quickly and creates the layered structure most useful for wildlife. The same staggered double-row method applies when gapping up an established hedge rather than planting a new one.
  • Planting depth and firming: the cell goes in at the same depth it sat in the tray, the root ball seated firmly with no air pockets, and the plant heeled in well so the first frost cannot lift it.
  • Weed suppression around newly planted stock: a mulch mat or a layer of well-rotted material around the base keeps grass and weeds from competing with the new root systems in their first growing season, which is usually the make-or-break period.
  • Watering in after planting, and the importance of checking soil moisture through the first dry spell after establishment, whenever that falls.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Heathwood are a specialist native hedge nursery and the video is excellent for technique. The species detail specific to this part of Kent is what it cannot provide.

Chalk slopes versus valley floor. Bridge sits where the North Downs chalk meets the Nailbourne valley bottom. The slopes running up toward Barham, Kingston and Lower Hardres are free-draining, alkaline chalk. The valley floor through Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne and Bishopsbourne is heavier and wetter in a wet winter, especially when the Nailbourne itself is flowing. These two environments suit slightly different species mixes, and getting that choice right in year one makes a significant difference to how the hedge looks in year five.

For chalk slopes, hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is the backbone of most North Downs field hedges for good reason: it is tolerant of thin, alkaline ground and drought once established, and it berries heavily. Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) thickens a hedge fast and provides the dense, spiny structure that fieldfare and redwing rely on for winter shelter in the valley. Field maple (Acer campestre), the UK's only native maple, is genuinely at home on the chalk and provides good autumn colour and winged seed for finches. Wayfaring tree (Viburnum lantana) and spindle (Euonymus europaeus) are both chalk specialists, native to this landscape, and both provide fruit taken by garden birds in autumn. These two species are relatively unusual in nursery-mix hedging but are exactly the kind of local-character choice that makes a Nailbourne valley hedge look right rather than generic. Hazel (Corylus avellana) is worth adding to either chalk-slope or valley-floor mix; see the hazel management guide for how to keep it in proportion within a clipped boundary hedge.

For the valley floor, hawthorn and blackthorn again form the core. Elder (Sambucus nigra) does well in the wetter, more fertile ground and provides both early flower and heavy berry crops for warblers and thrushes. Dogwood (Cornus sanguinea) is another genuine valley-floor native on chalk downland: it colours well in autumn and produces berries taken by woodpigeon and starling. Avoid planting in standing water; if the ground is waterlogged in late autumn, it is worth waiting until the soil has drained before planting rather than heeling stock into a saturated trench.

Wildlife value in context. The Nailbourne valley sits within the Kent Downs Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty and is part of a hedgerow landscape that extends from the old Roman road through Bridge, along the valley through Bishopsbourne and Kingston, and up onto the open downland above Barham. Hedgerows here are not just boundaries, they are the wildlife corridors that connect the chalk downland to the valley floor, and the species composition matters for that function. A hawthorn and blackthorn hedge with field maple, spindle and dog rose woven through it provides nesting cover, berry crops and insect habitat across the whole calendar year. For a formal stand-alone boundary hedge that still contributes a heavy autumn berry crop, Rosa rugosa handles the chalk well and produces large hips taken by fieldfares and redwings through October and November, though it is not a UK native. A hedge planted purely for density and privacy, with only fast-growing non-native species, fills in quickly but does not hold that function.

Cell-grown timing around Bridge. The broader planting window for cell-grown stock, roughly September to April, is genuinely useful here. September and October planting on the chalk slopes gives roots time to anchor before the first frosts and takes advantage of autumn rainfall. On the valley floor, I would be cautious about planting in January or February when the Nailbourne may be flowing and the ground is at its wettest: stock planted into saturated, cold soil will sit without establishing until spring, by which point bare-root stock planted in November would already have a season of root growth. For valley-floor sites I tend to prefer September to November or March, using cell-grown stock to hit those windows when bare-root availability does not line up.

The nesting law and new hedges. A newly planted hedge does not carry an immediate nesting risk in the same way an established one does, but section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 applies as soon as the hedge starts providing cover: it is an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while that nest is in use. The safe cutting window is outside 1 March to 31 August. In practice, a well-planted native hedge needs no cutting at all in its first two or three seasons: the initial formative cut, reducing the top growth by a third to a half to encourage bushy growth from low down, is usually done at planting or shortly afterwards, well within the dormancy period. After that, the hedge is left to thicken before any maintenance cutting begins. See the nesting-law timing guide for the full legal position.

Conservation area planting. New planting does not itself require notice under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. The notice requirement applies to the removal or cutting of trees and larger shrubs within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, not to new planting. If you are replanting where a hedge was removed, it is worth checking whether the removal side was handled correctly before assuming planting alone covers the position. Canterbury City Council administers the CA rules for Bridge parish.
