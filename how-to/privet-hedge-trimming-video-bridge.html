Privet hedge trimming: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Privet is one of the most common garden boundary hedges in the villages around Bridge. You will find it lining front gardens and side boundaries throughout Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne and Bishopsbourne, as well as in the older plots along the Watling Street itself. This guide takes a real UK privet trimming video and adds what a general demonstration cannot cover: the nesting-law timing, the quirk of wild privet being native to North Downs chalk, and why a privet on the valley floor around the Nailbourne grows faster than one on the chalk slope above.

The video below is from Scott Ellwood Gardening, the YouTube channel of SE Landscaping, a professional groundcare company established in 2005 and based in the north of England. The work shown is a professional standard job on an overgrown garden privet hedge, and the approach transfers directly to the kind of established privet boundary you find in the Bridge CT4 area.

Watch first: How To Trim an Overgrown Privet Hedge

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=dXzMk5Ccpho · Channel: Scott Ellwood Gardening (SE Landscaping)

What the video shows

The video works through an established privet that has been allowed to run significantly past its intended size, which is a very common situation. It covers:

  • Assessing the hedge before starting: reading how much growth has been added and how far back the cut needs to go to restore a workable shape, without exposing bare old wood that will not regenerate. Privet is more tolerant of hard cuts than most conifers, but there is still a point past which new growth will not break from old stem.
  • Working the face cut with a powered hedge trimmer, using a sweeping action from the base upward and keeping a consistent batter (the sides narrower at the top than the base) so the lower portions of the hedge stay in the light and do not die back.
  • Dealing with the top cut: bringing a significantly overgrown privet back to a lower running height without leaving a blunt, unsightly crown. The video demonstrates the difference between a clean flat top and a ragged one, and how to hold the trimmer to achieve it.
  • Managing the arisings. Privet produces a significant volume of clippings, particularly on an overgrown hedge, and the video shows a practical approach to clearing and removing the material rather than leaving it at the hedge base where it can hold moisture and encourage disease.

What the video does not go into is when to do this work, and with privet that question is slightly more nuanced than it is with purely structural species like hornbeam or beech.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The nesting law, and privet's flowers. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. The reliable nesting window runs from 1 March through to the end of August, sometimes extending into early September with late broods. That is straightforward enough. The additional consideration with privet specifically is that Ligustrum ovalifolium (garden privet) flowers in June and July, producing small white flower clusters that are a genuine food source for hoverflies and bees in midsummer. A cut taken in late June or early July removes those flowers mid-season, which is not illegal but is worth bearing in mind. My own practice is to take the main annual cut in late August or early September: after the nesting period, after the flowers are spent, and while the weather is still reliable enough to work cleanly. A second lighter tidy is usually possible in May, just before the nesting season reaches its peak, if the hedge has grown significantly over winter. The nesting-law timing guide covers the legal position in full.

Garden privet versus wild privet on North Downs chalk. Almost every privet hedge around Bridge is Ligustrum ovalifolium, the semi-evergreen Japanese garden privet introduced to UK horticulture in the nineteenth century. But Ligustrum vulgare, the wild or common privet, is genuinely native to chalk and limestone downland across southern England and grows in hedgerows and scrub on the North Downs naturally. If you are planting a new screen on the chalk slope above Bridge toward Lower Hardres or Patrixbourne, wild privet is worth considering as part of a native mix: it is less vigorous than the garden species, tolerates drought and shallow chalk soil better, and its berries (which are toxic, worth knowing if the property is used by children or dogs) support birds in autumn. It is mentioned in the planting guide for North Downs chalk alongside hawthorn and blackthorn as a native chalk-downland species.

Chalk slope versus valley floor growth rates. Garden privet on the chalk slopes above Bridge, in the drier, thinner soil toward Patrixbourne Road or the lane up to Lower Hardres, grows at a measurably slower rate than the same species in the damper, deeper alluvial soil of the Nailbourne valley floor. On well-drained chalk, one main cut a year is often sufficient to hold a maintained garden privet. On valley-floor plots in lower Bridge or toward Bekesbourne, where the water table is higher and the soil retains moisture, privet can put on enough growth to need a third light trim to keep it within bounds. That difference in vigour is worth factoring into any quote: a privet hedge that looks the same size as its neighbour on the chalk above will produce more arisings and take longer to clear.

Hard pruning a very overgrown privet. Privet is one of the more forgiving species when a hedge has been left for several years and needs a significant reduction rather than routine maintenance. Unlike Leylandii (which will not regenerate from brown wood) or yew (which requires a staged approach over two seasons), privet can tolerate being cut back quite hard in late winter, late February into early March, before the nesting season begins. I would survey any privet hedge before quoting a hard reduction, because the tolerance varies between older established plants and younger ones, and between specimens in full sun and those on a shaded north-facing boundary. The key rule is the same as for any species: do not cut back past the point where green foliage is visible on the stem. You can be aggressive with privet, but not unlimited. For context on what a severe reduction involves in practice, the hard pruning laurel guide covers the comparable approach for a different fast-growing species.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area. Most garden privet hedges around Bridge fall well under the 75mm stem threshold that triggers a six-week notice requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 for trees in a conservation area. A maintained privet hedge is not a tree in the legal sense and does not require notice. However, if a privet in or immediately bordering the Bifrons Park CA has been allowed to grow into a multi-stem specimen with stems approaching or exceeding that 75mm threshold, the position changes: those stems, if they are within or immediately adjacent to the CA boundary, need the same six-week notification to Canterbury City Council as any other woody stem of that size. I check this before quoting on any job near Patrixbourne Road or the older estate boundaries in the area.

Berries: a practical note. Privet berries (the small black drupes that follow the flowers from late summer into autumn) are toxic to people and dogs. This is not a legal point, but it is worth mentioning if the hedge borders a garden used by children or where a dog might graze at the base. The berries ripen in September and October, so any significant late-autumn cut will remove them anyway. The birds will take whatever remains over winter.
