Traditional hedge laying, South of England style: a video companion for Bridge.

Hedge laying is a genuinely old craft, and it is still the right answer for a gappy, leggy field-boundary hedge around Bridge and Patrixbourne, more often than a straight cut-and-hope. This guide walks through a real demonstration of the South of England style, then covers what the video cannot, which is how that technique lands on a Nailbourne-valley hawthorn hedge specifically.

Watch first: Traditional Hedge Laying in the South of England Style

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=iGncS_lojlI

What the video shows

The film demonstrates traditional hedge laying in the South of England style, the regional pattern used across Kent, Sussex and Surrey, distinct from the Midland bullock or Welsh border styles you will find in other counties' hedging videos. It covers:

  • Selecting and thinning a mature, gappy hedgerow before laying begins.
  • Cutting the pleachers, the near-horizontal partial cuts through each stem that let it bend without snapping, the defining technique of hedge laying.
  • Laying the pleachers down at the South of England style's characteristic angle, with hazel stakes driven in at intervals to hold the line.
  • Binding the top with hazel or willow binders to lock the stakes and pleachers together into a stock-proof barrier.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The video is a general South of England demonstration, not specific to any one parish, so here is what changes on the ground around Bridge.

Species mix. The field-boundary hedges out towards Patrixbourne and Lower Hardres are overwhelmingly hawthorn and blackthorn, with field maple and some hazel worked in, the same species this style was developed for, so the technique transfers cleanly. Where a hedge has a significant proportion of elder or bramble tangled through it, expect more clearance work before a single pleacher gets cut. Once the laid hedge is re-established and back in routine maintenance, the hawthorn hedge trimming guide covers the follow-on annual cut, including the timing constraints that apply to hawthorn field boundaries specifically.

Timing and the nesting law. Hedge laying is invasive work on the whole structure of the hedge, not a light trim, so it falls squarely under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The safe window is 1 September to the end of February, well outside the 1 March to 31 August nesting season, and the traditional laying season (November to March, when sap is down and stems bend without splitting) sits comfortably inside that legal window anyway.

Chalk vs valley floor. On the downland above Bridge, where the soil is thin and free-draining, a laid hawthorn hedge re-establishes and thickens at the base reliably within two growing seasons. Down in the Nailbourne valley bottom, where cold air pools and the ground holds more moisture, regrowth is often slower to start in spring but catches up over summer, and I plan the follow-up formative cut a season later than I would on the downs.

Conservation-area boundary. If the hedge in question borders the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, check the stem diameters before laying starts, anything over 75mm at 1.5m needs a section-211 notice to Canterbury City Council, the same threshold that applies to ordinary pruning.
