Pruning cornus (dogwood) for winter colour: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Dogwood grown for winter stem colour is one of the most rewarding shrubs to manage, and the Crocus.co.uk guide makes the coppicing technique clear. What it cannot do is tell you how that technique interacts with the native common dogwood (Cornus sanguinea) found throughout the Nailbourne valley's field boundary hedges, where the late-winter coppicing window sits against the nesting season that opens on 1 March, or when to check a larger specimen against the Bifrons Park Conservation Area rules before cutting.

Watch first: How-to: Prune Dogwoods (Cornus) – Crocus.co.uk

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=XuLFs9xw-Ds

What the video shows

Helen, Crocus.co.uk's plant doctor, explains why cornus grown for winter stem colour needs regular coppicing and walks through both the hard method and the gentler rotation approach. The core points:

  • The vivid red, orange or yellow colour comes from young stems. First-year growth carries the brightest colour; older stems grey and harden over two or three seasons, losing their display value. Coppicing removes that old wood and forces a flush of new, brightly coloured stems each year.
  • The standard technique for established coloured-stem cultivars is to cut all stems back to 5-7 cm from the ground in late winter or very early spring, while the plant is still dormant. The plant responds with strong new shoots that colour up well by the following winter.
  • For younger plants or less vigorous specimens, a rolling rotation is safer: removing the oldest third of stems to the base each year, rather than taking the entire above-ground framework in one go. This maintains some structure while still refreshing the display each season.
  • The video demonstrates the technique on an established Cornus alba cultivar and shows the contrast between the dull older stems being removed and the vivid young ones left or expected.
  • Helen is clear on a distinction that matters: this hard coppicing approach is for stem-colour varieties (C. alba, C. sanguinea, C. sericea and their cultivars), not for flowering dogwoods such as C. florida and C. kousa, which are grown for spring bract display and should be left alone or only lightly tidied after flowering.

What the video does not address is the native hedgerow context for Cornus sanguinea in this part of Kent, the narrow timing window between the right dormancy period and the legal nesting season, or what to check before cutting a larger cornus in a conservation area.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Cornus sanguinea: a North Downs native in the valley hedges. The common dogwood is one of the characteristic species of the North Downs chalk downland and appears throughout the field boundary hedges of the Nailbourne valley, alongside hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel and field maple. It is not an ornamental planting: in a Patrixbourne or Bishopsbourne field boundary it is part of the hedge structure, contributing dense growth, autumn red and orange leaf colour, and clusters of black berries in September and October. Those berries are taken by woodpigeon, starling and fieldfare moving through the valley in autumn, and the dense stem structure provides winter cover for wrens and hedgerow invertebrates. When managing native hedgerows that include cornus sanguinea, the goal is usually to keep it in proportion within the hedge structure rather than to maximise stem colour. The coppicing-for-colour technique in the video is primarily relevant to ornamental cultivars used in garden boundaries, not to native hedgerow stock.

Ornamental cultivars around Bridge. In village gardens and newer boundary plantings, the stem-colour cultivars are common: Cornus alba 'Sibirica' (vivid red stems), Cornus sanguinea 'Midwinter Fire' (orange to yellow stems), and Cornus sericea 'Flaviramea' (yellow-green stems) all appear regularly in Bridge and the Nailbourne villages. These are the plants the Crocus.co.uk video is primarily aimed at, and the coppicing technique applies directly. If you have one of these at the base of a garden boundary or as a specimen in a border, the late-winter coppice is straightforward once the timing question is sorted out.

The nesting-law timing: the window is narrow. The standard coppicing time for coloured-stem cornus is late winter to very early spring, targeting the last dormant weeks before new growth begins. In this part of Kent, that typically means late February to early March. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird, and the nesting season effectively opens on 1 March. Some years, in a mild late February, early-nesting wrens and robins begin on suitable low, dense structure before that date. That leaves a real but narrow window: I carry out cornus coppicing in the last two weeks of February, and I do a nest check the morning of the cut on any low, dense structure. The check takes five minutes and it is not optional. Delaying into March is not the safe choice: the plant is pushing new growth by then and the coppice cut is less effective, while the nesting risk is higher, not lower. For the full nesting-law position, see the nesting-law timing guide.

Native hedgerow cornus: manage for structure, not for stems. If the cornus sanguinea in a field boundary hedge has grown large after several missed years, coppicing it to the ground removes the berry display entirely for that season and can leave a gap in the hedge structure through the summer. For native hedgerow stock I tend to manage on a staggered rotation rather than hard coppicing: removing the oldest, most congested stems to the base and shortening the remainder as part of the boundary hedge cut, keeping the shrub in proportion without resetting it entirely. This is different from what the video demonstrates and is better matched to what the hedgerows of the Nailbourne valley actually need. For a mixed native hedge planting that includes cornus sanguinea, include it at roughly 10-15% of the species mix and expect to manage it with the annual hedge cut rather than a separate coppicing regime.

Berry crop or stem colour: different management, different timing. This is the key choice. If the reason for planting cornus was the winter stem display, coppice hard in late February and sacrifice most of the berry crop, since the plant will put its energy into new stem growth rather than berrying heavily. If the cornus is in a position where the autumn berries are the point, managing it less hard, allowing it to carry two or three years of wood, produces a heavier berry crop at the cost of some stem-colour intensity. The two objectives are not wholly compatible, and it is worth deciding which matters more before picking up the loppers.

Conservation area: when to check before cutting. Coloured-stem cornus grown in garden borders and kept on a regular coppicing cycle carries stems typically 10-20 mm in diameter, well below the 75 mm stem-diameter threshold at 1.5 m that triggers the six-week notification requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. That threshold applies to work in or bordering the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, administered by Canterbury City Council. However, cornus sanguinea that has been left unmanaged in an older garden boundary or estate planting, or native stock that has grown unchecked in a field hedge for a decade or more, can reach multi-stem trunks substantially above 75 mm at the base. If the material looks structural rather than shrubby, I measure at 1.5 m before booking the cut. For the notice process and what it involves, the conservation-area notice guide covers it in full. For a standard maintained garden specimen on a regular coppicing cycle, the threshold is not a live concern.
