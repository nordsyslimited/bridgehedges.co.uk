Leylandii hedge trimming: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Leylandii (x Cupressocyparis leylandii) is the most planted garden-boundary screen in Britain, which also makes it one of the most frequently argued about. best4hedging, one of the UK's largest specialist hedging plant suppliers, have put together a clear and honest guide to the species. This article takes their video and adds what it cannot: the nesting-season timing for this ground, how growth rate differs between Bridge's chalk slopes and the wetter Nailbourne valley floor, and the High Hedges legislation that specifically applies when Leylandii is the species involved.

Watch first: A focus on Leylandii hedging (best4hedging)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=IImv7urAPMI  ·  Channel: best4hedging

What the video shows

best4hedging is a major UK online hedging plant retailer with a knowledge base built around their own stock and customer questions. Their Leylandii guide covers the species honestly, which means it starts with the thing you most need to understand before you plant or maintain one. The video demonstrates:

  • Why Leylandii became the default boundary screen. Fast growth (roughly 60-90cm per year once established on a reasonable soil, faster on wetter ground), dense evergreen coverage that works as a windbreak and privacy screen from an early age, and tolerance of most soil types. The video makes clear these advantages come with an obligation: a hedge that grows this fast needs managing from the start, not when it has already got away from you.
  • The single most important fact about Leylandii: it will not regrow from brown wood. Unlike cherry laurel, hornbeam or any native deciduous species, Leylandii does not regenerate from old, bare wood. If you cut into the brown interior of the hedge, those sections stay brown permanently. There is no recovering a Leylandii hedge that has been cut back hard into the dead zone. Every cut, whether an annual maintenance trim or a height reduction, must stay within the living green growth. Early in the video the channel is clear about this, which is the right starting point.
  • Why regular trimming is not optional. Because every cut must stay in the green, the size of each cut is limited by how much green growth has accumulated since the last trim. A hedge trimmed annually has a metre or more of manageable green zone; a hedge left for three or four seasons may have only the outermost surface of growth in the green, with the rest already gone brown. Letting a Leylandii hedge go is not the same as letting a beech or hornbeam go: you cannot simply cut it back hard later and wait for recovery.
  • Basic trimming technique. Working along the sides first, then the top. Keeping a slight batter (wider at the base, narrower at the top) ensures the lower growth gets light and the hedge does not thin out at the base over time. The video shows a typical garden hedge rather than a large commercial planting, which is the relevant scale for most properties around Bridge.
  • Training a new hedge from planting. The video notes that regular light trimming from the start, taking the tips back to encourage branching rather than single-stem vertical growth, produces a denser hedge faster than leaving it to grow freely. A Leylandii hedge started this way is markedly easier to maintain at a controlled height long term.

The video does not address the nesting law, the growth differences between chalk and valley-floor ground, the High Hedges legislation, or what to do when a Leylandii hedge has already exceeded the point where easy reduction is possible. Those are the parts worth reading further.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The nesting-season window. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. Leylandii is a year-round nesting species: its dense, evergreen interior provides cover in winter as well as summer, and it is used by a wider range of birds than most garden hedges. The core nesting period runs from 1 March through to the end of August. My practice with any Leylandii hedge around Bridge is to do the main annual trim in early to mid-September, after nesting has reliably closed and while the hedge is still in active growth. A second light trim in late spring, if the hedge genuinely warrants it, should be preceded by a visual check for active nests immediately before starting. For more detail on how the legal timing works and what a proper nest check involves, see the guide to hedge trimming timing and the nesting law.

Chalk versus valley floor: the growth-rate difference. Bridge sits at the northern edge of the North Downs chalk. On the free-draining chalk slopes above the village and along the lanes towards Patrixbourne and Lower Hardres, Leylandii grows somewhat more slowly than the standard 60-90cm per year figure suggests: the soil drains freely and summers can be dry. This is not a problem; a slightly slower hedge is a more manageable hedge, and one annual trim will usually keep it well within the green zone. On the heavier, moister soils of the Nailbourne valley floor, particularly towards Bishopsbourne and Bekesbourne, growth rates are higher, sometimes well over 90cm in a good season. A valley-floor Leylandii hedge that gets only one trim every two years will very likely outgrow its green zone before the second cut arrives. I would plan two cuts a year for a valley-floor hedge: a light trim in late May or early June after the nesting check, and the main cut in September.

The High Hedges law, and why it particularly applies to Leylandii. Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 gives a neighbour the right to complain to the local authority about an evergreen or semi-evergreen hedge over 2 metres that is adversely affecting the reasonable enjoyment of their property. Canterbury City Council administers this for the Bridge parish. Leylandii triggers this route more than any other hedge species because its growth rate means that a hedge planted as a modest 1.5-metre screen can pass 2 metres in a single season if not cut. The legislation applies regardless of how long the hedge has been there and regardless of whether it was planted before the Act came into force. If a complaint is accepted, the council issues a formal notice specifying a "remedial height" to which the hedge owner must reduce it. I have covered the full process, the timescales and the costs in the guide to your neighbour's high hedge. The practical lesson here is that keeping a Leylandii hedge trimmed annually is far cheaper than the process that follows when it is not.

Conservation-area notice. Leylandii can reach the 75mm stem-diameter threshold at 1.5m height in under a decade on reasonable ground. Any hedge within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area that has reached that size requires six weeks' prior notice to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 before any works can begin. Routine annual trimming of an established hedge that has been maintained within the green zone rarely triggers this threshold, but any significant reduction of an overgrown hedge or removal of individual specimens does. I check the address against the CA boundary before quoting for reduction work.

What to do if a Leylandii hedge has got away from you. The honest answer is that the options narrow considerably once the hedge is in the brown. If the hedge has grown beyond the point where there is a realistic green zone to cut back to, and the sides or top have already been cut into the brown in previous years, a patchwork of dead areas is likely permanent. In that situation the practical options are: accept the current size and maintain it going forward without trying to reduce it; reduce height to the point where green growth still exists at the new top, accepting that the sides may carry some dead patches; or remove the hedge and replant with a species better suited to the space. If the issue is specifically neighbour light rather than aesthetics, laurel is worth considering as a replacement since it regenerates freely from old wood (see the guide to hard pruning a laurel screen). I will say this clearly when surveying a job, rather than booking a reduction and discovering the problem once the cut starts.
