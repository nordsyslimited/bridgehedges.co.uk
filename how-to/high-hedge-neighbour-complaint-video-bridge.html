Your neighbour's high hedge: the law, Canterbury's process, and Bridge, Kent.

Disputes about a neighbour's overshadowing evergreen hedge are one of the most common things people contact me about from Bridge, Patrixbourne and Bekesbourne. There is a specific statutory route for these disputes, and it runs through Canterbury City Council. This guide takes a UK garden law video by BlackBeltBarrister, a practising barrister in England and Wales with one of the better-known legal explanation channels on YouTube, and adds the one layer the general overview cannot: how Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 actually works in practice for a property in Bridge, and what the nesting-law and conservation-area complications mean for the physical work that comes out of it.

What the video shows

Daniel ShenSmith is a practising barrister in England and Wales. The BlackBeltBarrister channel translates legal principles into plain English for a general audience and has well over half a million subscribers. This particular video covers the general framework for garden boundary disputes, working through what each party can and cannot do without involving lawyers or the council. It explains:

  • The right to cut back overhanging branches and encroaching roots to the boundary line. This is a self-help remedy that does not require the neighbour's permission, though the cut material legally belongs to the neighbour and should be offered back. The video makes clear that going further than the boundary line is trespass.
  • What "nuisance" means in a legal context. Not all annoyances are actionable. The video explains the threshold: something must cause a material interference with the use and enjoyment of land, or cause physical damage, before the law of nuisance applies. A hedge that blocks some light may not meet the threshold for private nuisance even if it is genuinely irritating.
  • When formal routes become relevant. The video outlines the distinction between informal resolution, solicitors, and the role councils can play under specific statutory schemes. It notes that council involvement under a specific regime is often the more proportionate route for hedge disputes rather than litigation.
  • The limits of self-help. The video flags that acting without legal advice can expose a homeowner to a counter-claim, particularly if works damage a neighbour's tree or hedge beyond what the boundary line strictly permits.

The video gives the general legal landscape for England and Wales. What it does not provide is the specific statutory process that applies to high evergreen hedges: Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, which is the mechanism most relevant to the disputes I see in the lanes around Bridge.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 (the high hedges regime). The BlackBeltBarrister video covers the general law of nuisance and self-help rights, but for disputes specifically about the height of evergreen hedges blocking light, Parliament created a dedicated statutory remedy. Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, which came into force in June 2005, allows a homeowner to make a formal complaint to their local council about a neighbouring evergreen hedge that is adversely affecting the reasonable enjoyment of their property.

The statutory definition is specific. A "high hedge" under Part 8 is a barrier to light or access formed wholly or predominantly by a line of two or more evergreen or semi-evergreen trees or shrubs that rises above 2m above ground level. A single tree does not qualify, regardless of height. Deciduous species (beech, hornbeam, hawthorn) do not qualify even in leafless winter, because they are not "evergreen" in the statutory sense. The most common qualifying hedges I encounter around Bridge and the villages south towards Barham and Kingston are Leylandii (x Cupressocyparis leylandii) and Lawson cypress (Chamaecyparis lawsoniana), typically grown as fast-screening hedges in the 1980s and 1990s and left to reach several metres. Dense laurel screens can also qualify. The laurel reduction guide covers the physical work for that species.

Canterbury City Council's role. For properties in Bridge parish, Canterbury City Council administers Part 8. Before Canterbury will accept a formal complaint, the affected person must demonstrate that all reasonable steps have been taken to resolve the dispute directly with the hedge owner: a written request to reduce the hedge, evidence of the attempts made, and preferably a record of the neighbour's response. The council is not a first resort: it will not accept a complaint unless direct resolution has been genuinely tried and failed.

If direct resolution fails, the affected person submits a formal complaint to Canterbury's planning department. The council then investigates and decides whether the hedge is adversely affecting the reasonable enjoyment of the complainant's property. If it is satisfied that it is, it has the power to issue a "remedial notice" requiring the hedge to be reduced to a specified height and maintained at that height going forward. Check Canterbury City Council's current planning pages for the complaint fee before submitting: the fee has changed over time and I would not advise using a figure from memory or from an online summary that may be out of date.

How Leylandii responds to a hard cut, and why this matters for compliance. A remedial notice will specify a target height, and the hedge owner must comply. The physical reality of a Leylandii that has been left to grow to six or eight metres is that cutting it back hard to old brown wood will not regenerate new growth the way a laurel or a yew would. Leylandii will not break from old wood: cut beyond the green growth zone and the affected sections will die brown and remain dead. The practical advice when reducing a large Leylandii is to take it back to within the most recent season's green growth and no further, accepting that the target height may have to be achieved over two or three annual cuts rather than in one pass. This is a conversation I have with clients before agreeing to the physical works.

The nesting-season complication. Any reduction of an established evergreen hedge, whether as part of a Part 8 remedy or by private agreement, must work around the nesting season. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. For a dense conifer screen, which is a favoured nesting habitat for goldcrest, wren, chaffinch and long-tailed tit, the active season runs from 1 March through to the end of August. Any works on a qualifying hedge should either be scheduled outside this window (September through to late February) or preceded by a nest survey. I cover the survey steps in the nesting-law guide. If a remedial notice specifies works but does not specify a timing window, the hedge owner would still commit an offence if an active nest is disturbed: the civil notice does not override the criminal wildlife law.

The Bifrons Park Conservation Area. If the hedge or any of its individual trees fall within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, works to stems above 75mm diameter at 1.5m above ground require six weeks' prior notification to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990, before any physical works begin. A Part 8 remedial notice does not override this: both requirements apply in parallel if the hedge is within the CA. I survey the stem sizes and check the CA boundary before confirming any booking on a hedge near the old estate boundaries around Patrixbourne Road where the CA line runs.

What I can and cannot advise on. As a working contractor rather than a planning solicitor, I can assess a hedge, confirm whether it is the sort of species that falls under Part 8, advise on the physical reduction technique, and book the works within the right seasonal window. The legal steps, writing to the neighbour, making the formal complaint to Canterbury, and responding to a remedial notice, are for the landowner to take up directly with Canterbury City Council or, if the dispute is contested, with a solicitor. The GOV.UK guidance on high hedges is a clear and reliable starting point for the process; the link is in the Sources below.
