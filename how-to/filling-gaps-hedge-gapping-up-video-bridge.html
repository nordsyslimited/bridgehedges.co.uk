Filling gaps in an established hedge: a video companion for Bridge and the Nailbourne valley.

Tom Hynes is a hedge-laying expert whose practical film work with Moor Meadows demonstrates traditional techniques that are genuinely hard to find clearly explained anywhere else. The third film in the series focuses on what happens when an established hedge develops a gap: stems that have died back, been damaged by grazing animals or vehicles, or simply thinned out over decades until the hedge is no longer continuous. The video shows how to fill that gap with new planting, working young whips into the existing structure so the repair becomes permanent. This article takes that demonstration and adds what it cannot cover: the specific decisions that follow from working on the North Downs chalk or the Nailbourne valley floor around Bridge.

Watch first: Part 3 - Curing a Gap (Moor Meadows, feat. Tom Hynes)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=aAjairrhk4M  ·  Channel: Moor Meadows

What the video shows

Tom Hynes works through the standard approach to closing a gap in an established hedge by planting young whips into prepared ground within the gap. The film is around three minutes, so it covers the essentials without detailing every variation. The key stages it demonstrates:

  • Preparing the planting strip. Before any whip goes in, the ground in the gap is cleared of grass and competing vegetation. A planting strip at least 1.5 metres wide gives each whip enough room to establish without being throttled by grass roots in its first season.
  • Planting in a staggered double row. A single row of whips leaves a line that is easy to push through once the plants establish. Two rows, staggered so the plants in the back row fill the spaces between the front row, produce the dense, impenetrable base the hedge needs to become stock-proof again.
  • Depth and handling for bare-root whips. Tom Hynes shows the correct planting depth and why firm heeling-in matters: a whip planted too shallow will rock in wind and break the fine root contact with the soil that allows it to take up water from the first week.
  • Protecting new plants. Young whips need protection from grazing before they develop enough bark to resist browsing. The video addresses the stock-proof requirement directly, which is the primary concern on working farms.
  • The timescale for closure. The film sets reasonable expectations: a well-planted gap in a hawthorn hedge will close enough to become stock-proof within three to five growing seasons, and the full hedge structure another season or two beyond that.

The method shown applies directly to both field-boundary and garden hedges. The differences that matter around Bridge come from the soil, the species, the season and the animals present, none of which the film can account for.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Species for North Downs chalk. The old field-boundary hedges that run across the chalk slopes between Bridge, Patrixbourne and Lower Hardres are typically a mix of hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna), blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), dogwood (Cornus sanguinea), wayfaring tree (Viburnum lantana) and spindle (Euonymus europaeus), with occasional field maple (Acer campestre) and wild privet (Ligustrum vulgare). When gapping up one of these hedges, the right approach is to replant with the same species that already make up the hedge structure. Buying from a nursery that specifies British native provenance and ideally south-east England provenance gives plants that are genetically matched to local conditions and establishes better on chalk-derived soils than continental stock. The mixed wildlife hedge planting guide covers species selection in more detail for new planting, much of which applies to a gapping-up job as well.

Species for the Nailbourne valley floor. The valley floor near Bishopsbourne and Barham is a different proposition from the chalk slopes above. The Nailbourne is a winterbourne: it runs when the water table rises through autumn and winter, and the valley floor can carry standing water for weeks in a wet season. Hedges alongside the lower valley floor need a species mix that tolerates seasonal waterlogging. Alder (Alnus glutinosa), goat willow (Salix caprea) and elder (Sambucus nigra) are appropriate for valley-floor gaps where the chalk-slope species would struggle. A gap in a hedge at the base of a chalk slope, where the soil shifts from drained chalk rubble to heavier valley-floor ground, often needs two different species mixes in the same run of planting.

Timing and the nesting season. Bare-root whips are planted in dormancy, which runs from roughly late October to early March. This aligns with the window before the nesting season opens under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: nesting activity for most garden birds and hedge-nesting species starts in early March, and the active nesting period runs through to the end of August. The planting itself does not trigger the Act, but any clearing of vegetation from the gap or the hedge margins should be done before nesting begins, or should follow a careful nest-check if done later in the season. The nesting law and cutting timing guide covers the full legal position. Where the Countryside Stewardship BN7 grant for hedgerow gapping-up applies to land around Bridge, the scheme specifies that grant-funded work should be completed between 1 November and 31 March.

Deer and rabbit protection on chalk slopes. The video addresses livestock pressure, which is the primary concern on the farms shown. Around Bridge and the lanes to Patrixbourne, the pressure is different: deer from the woodland east of the village occasionally browse young growth in winter, and rabbits are a more consistent problem along open chalk slopes. A plastic spiral guard around each whip, pushed 5 to 7 cm into the soil so rabbits cannot push it aside, gives adequate protection for the first two or three seasons until stems have developed enough bark to deter browsing. A short run of rabbit netting pegged across the gap works as an alternative if the number of whips makes individual guards impractical. On the valley floor near Bishopsbourne, hares are a bigger concern than rabbits; spirals are usually sufficient for hare browsing at ankle height.

Soil preparation on chalk. The chalk pan can sit close to the surface on Bridge's slopes. Breaking it up with a fork or a bar before planting gives roots room to move into fractured chalk below. There is no need to dig a large pit: a planting-spade depth with the chalk pan broken at the base is usually sufficient for a bare-root whip. Adding mycorrhizal granules to the planting hole can help on chalk-derived soil where the fungal community in the disturbed gap is less established than in the settled hedge base either side. There is no benefit to adding compost to a chalk-soil planting hole; it creates a soft pocket that roots fill quickly and then struggle to exit into the surrounding chalk. Working back in with the existing soil is better.

Aftercare and the most common cause of failure. The most frequent cause of failure after gapping-up is not species choice or planting technique: it is grass competition in the first season. A mulch mat or a run of weed-suppressing membrane around each whip for the first two growing seasons reduces competition enough that most plants establish without supplementary watering. On the valley floor near Bishopsbourne, drought stress in the first summer is rarely a problem: the ground stays damp long enough into spring that most whips are established before any dry period arrives. On the chalk slopes above Bridge, a newly planted gap may need a bucket of water in the first summer if the season is dry and the chalk has dried out around the roots. The longer-term restoration question, when the gap is more than a few metres and the hedge on each side has also thinned out, is often better answered by hedge laying rather than planting alone. The hedge laying guide covers the South of England method and the winter timing that keeps it inside the nesting law.
