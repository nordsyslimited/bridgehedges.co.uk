Forsythia hedge pruning: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Forsythia is one of the most common shrubs in the older gardens of Bridge, Patrixbourne and Bishopsbourne: the bright yellow flowers in late February and March are a reliable early-spring marker along the village lanes and in the larger plots that line the old Watling Street corridor. The trouble with forsythia is that cutting it at the right time, immediately after flowering, puts you almost exactly at the start of the nesting season. This guide works through David Domoney's pruning demonstration, then covers what the timing tension looks like on this ground specifically.

Watch first: When And How To Prune Your Forsythia Shrub, David Domoney

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=2ZCFyc9qEXo · Channel: David Domoney

What the video shows

David Domoney is a Chartered Horticulturalist and one of the UK's most familiar garden media figures, known for ITV's Love Your Garden and his long-running role as a resident expert on This Morning. His forsythia video addresses the most common mistake home gardeners make with the plant, which is cutting it at the wrong time of year and losing the following spring's flower. It covers:

  • Why timing is everything. Forsythia flowers on the previous year's growth: the long arching shoots that grew through spring and summer carry the flower buds that open the following February or March. If you cut those shoots in autumn or winter, as you would with most deciduous hedging, you remove the buds and lose the display. The video establishes clearly that forsythia pruning happens immediately after flowering, while the shrub is still in leaf flush, not in the dormant season.
  • The one-third rule and renovation cuts. Domoney demonstrates taking out roughly one third of the oldest stems each year at or near the base, encouraging new growth from low down in the plant. For an overgrown, leggy forsythia that has been cut at the wrong time for years and has lost its lower growth, a harder renovation cut taken over two or three seasons, progressively removing the oldest stems, is shown as the recovery strategy.
  • How much to take off young growth. After removing old stems at the base, the remaining stems are shortened back by roughly one third to encourage branching. The result is a bushy, well-branched plant with plenty of young wood to carry next spring's flowers.
  • What not to do. The video specifically covers the hedge-trimmer approach that leaves forsythia flat-topped and flowering only on the outer surface, which reduces both the display and the health of the plant over time. It recommends working with the natural arching shape rather than clipping it to a formal outline.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The video is a national gardening guide and doesn't address the local factors that change the picture around here.

The frost pocket and the flowering window. Forsythia is an early flowerer, and the Nailbourne valley floor is a genuine frost pocket. Cold air drains off the chalk slopes into the valley bottom on still, clear nights, and the open lower sections of Bridge and the neighbouring villages of Patrixbourne and Bekesbourne can record later frosts than the ridge above. In a year with a sharp late frost in early March, forsythia flowers on valley-floor gardens can be caught and browned off before they have fully opened, which shortens the display window and can make the timing of the post-flowering cut harder to judge. Gardens on the chalk slopes above the village, out towards Lower Hardres or on the higher land toward Barham, generally escape that early-spring frost risk and see the flowers open and hold better.

The nesting-law timing conflict. This is the central problem with forsythia pruning in the UK, and it is more acute than the video suggests for anyone working on a larger, established plant. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with the nesting season running from 1 March through to 31 August. Forsythia typically finishes flowering in Bridge in late March or early April, which is exactly when nesting is well underway. The correct approach is a visual inspection before starting: part the branches and look for active nests before putting a saw or secateurs near any established forsythia that has enough structure to shelter a nest. In practice, a forsythia cut in late March or April is lower risk than one in May or June because nesting density is still building, but it is not a zero-risk window, and checking costs very little. The nesting-law timing guide sets out what a defensible check looks like.

Forsythia kept as a hedge versus as a natural shrub. Many forsythias in Bridge have been maintained as flat-fronted hedges along boundaries rather than as free-standing shrubs, which is exactly the approach Domoney advises against for flower production. A forsythia hedge clipped with a trimmer every year eventually becomes a wall of old unproductive wood flowering only on the outer inch of growth. If you have inherited a forsythia hedge in this state and want to recover it, the renovation approach in the video works, but it needs to be done in the narrow post-flowering window and with the nesting check first. In a really dense, old forsythia hedge, multi-year staged renovation is less stressful for the plant and easier to manage within the nesting-law window than a single hard cut.

Conservation-area check. Forsythia kept as a boundary screen in an older property that borders the Bifrons Park Conservation Area can develop substantial stems over decades. Once any woody stem reaches 75mm diameter at 1.5m height, section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 requires six weeks' notice to Canterbury City Council before carrying out significant work. For most garden forsythias this threshold is not reached, but on older, neglected plants in walled gardens close to the historic estate boundaries it is worth checking before a major renovation cut.
