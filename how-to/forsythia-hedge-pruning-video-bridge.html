Forsythia hedge pruning: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

David Domoney is a UK Chartered Horticulturist who has presented gardening on ITV's This Morning for many years. His forsythia pruning video covers the one thing that trips up most garden owners with this plant: prune at the wrong time and you strip out the following year's flowers entirely. Forsythia is one of the most commonly planted ornamental hedges in domestic gardens around Bridge and the Nailbourne valley villages, and many of them have not seen a pair of loppers in a decade. This guide uses the video as a starting point, then adds the parts it cannot cover: the nesting-law complication that puts forsythia's optimal pruning window squarely inside the legal nesting season, and the behaviour of this plant on the free-draining chalk around Bridge.

Watch first: When And How To Prune Your Forsythia Shrub With David Domoney

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=2ZCFyc9qEXo  ·  Channel: David Domoney

What the video shows

Domoney's video is short and direct. It is aimed at domestic gardeners rather than contractors, but the core pruning logic is the same at any scale. The key points it covers:

  • Why timing is not optional. Forsythia is one of those plants where getting the pruning window wrong does not just reduce the display a little; it can remove every flower bud for the following spring. The plant flowers on wood it made the previous season. Prune before or just after flowering and you leave that old wood in place to produce buds. Prune in summer or autumn and you cut off the current season's new growth, which carries next year's buds.
  • The correct window: immediately after flowering. The flowers fall and the new leaves appear at roughly the same time, in late March to April in most of southern England. That brief overlap, when the last yellow petals are dropping and the first green leaves are showing, is the signal to prune. Waiting until the leaves are fully out is still acceptable. Waiting until July is not.
  • Annual maintenance: cut back and remove old wood. For an established plant being kept tidy, the approach is to shorten the shoots that have just flowered back to two or three buds from their base, and to remove a proportion of the oldest stems entirely at ground level each year. Taking out the oldest stems (the ones that have developed thick grey bark) makes room for vigorous new growth from the base, which is what will carry the flowers in future years. A forsythia left unpruned for several years builds up a tangle of old wood that produces fewer and fewer flowers.
  • Renovation of a neglected plant. If a forsythia hedge has been left to run for many years and has become a large, leggy structure flowering only at the tips, the video addresses this directly: it can be cut back hard, close to ground level, in late winter. The plant will reshoot strongly. The trade-off is one season without flowers. On a plant that has been producing almost nothing for years, that is an acceptable swap.
  • What the video does not address. As with most general pruning guides, timing in relation to nesting birds and the specific conditions of chalk soil are not covered. Those are the parts worth reading further.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The nesting-season timing conflict. The optimal pruning window for forsythia, immediately after flowering in April, falls inside the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting season. Section 1 of the Act makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while that nest is in use or being built. The active nesting period runs from 1 March to 31 August; in practice, the peak of nest-building and early broods is April and May, which is exactly when forsythia hedges should be pruned.

Forsythia is a genuinely popular nesting habitat. Its dense, multi-stemmed growth structure and the fact that it leafs out before pruning is due makes it attractive to blackbirds, robins and dunnocks, all of which nest early. I check forsythia hedges before I start on them in April, parting the stems in sections and listening as well as looking. An active nest found partway through means stopping the job for that section and returning in late July or August once the young have fledged. The nesting-law timing guide on this site covers the legal position and the nest-check process in full.

If a forsythia hedge is heavily overgrown and is being cut back hard as a renovation, the timing question becomes more flexible. Hard renovation can be done in late winter (February, before nesting begins in earnest), which avoids the April conflict entirely and still gives the plant a full growing season to recover. The downside is losing one year's flowers; the upside is working outside the peak nest period. For a hedge that has not flowered usefully for years, I generally recommend the late-winter renovation rather than the post-bloom cut, precisely because it sidesteps the nesting complication.

Forsythia on North Downs chalk. Forsythia is not fussy about soil chemistry and performs well on the free-draining, mildly alkaline chalk soils characteristic of the North Downs above Bridge, along the lanes toward Barham, Kingston and Lower Hardres. It tolerates dry summers better than many ornamental shrubs once established, partly because of its root depth on chalk. I have seen old forsythia hedges on the chalk slopes around Patrixbourne doing well with minimal attention beyond occasional renovation cuts.

Frost timing in the Nailbourne valley. The valley floor from Bridge through Bishopsbourne is a frost pocket: cold air drains downhill and settles in the valley bottom, which means late frosts in April are more common here than on the chalk slopes above. Forsythia flowers from late February through to April in a typical year, and frost can catch and damage the blooms on valley-floor plants slightly later than on the chalk slopes. This does not affect the pruning timing significantly, but it does mean that valley-floor gardeners may find the forsythia is still in reasonable flower a week or two after the chalk-slope plants have finished, so the pruning window shifts slightly later. The same frost-pocket effect that delays flowering also slightly compresses the window before peak nesting; checking carefully before starting is even more worthwhile on lower-lying sites.

Renovation and replanting decisions. Some forsythia hedges around Bridge have grown to three metres or more in height and width, often where they were planted as boundary hedging in the 1960s and 1970s and have been left to run. Hard renovation cuts them back to framework stumps and the plant usually responds with strong basal regrowth within one season. If a hedge has died back significantly in places and the remaining plants are too far apart to fill in, renovation combined with gap-filling (planting new bare-root or container-grown plants into the spaces) is worth considering. The bare-root planting guide on this site covers the general planting process; forsythia bare-root stock is planted the same way, in the November to March window.

Conservation area considerations. Forsythia is a shrub rather than a tree and does not typically produce stems that reach the 75mm diameter threshold at 1.5m above ground that triggers the six-week prior-notification requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 in the Bifrons Park Conservation Area. Very old, neglected forsythia that has been left to develop a framework of large old stems might conceivably approach that threshold, but in practice I have not seen a forsythia hedge in Bridge where this was a live concern. If you are in the CA and in any doubt, I check the stems before booking. Canterbury City Council administers the Bifrons Park CA notification requirement for the Bridge parish.
