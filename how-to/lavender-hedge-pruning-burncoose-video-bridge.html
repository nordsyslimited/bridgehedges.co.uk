Lavender hedge pruning: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Lavender is one of the most forgiving low hedging plants on well-drained chalk soil, and the North Downs ground around Bridge suits it well. Get the annual trim right and a lavender hedge stays dense and aromatic for a decade or more; get it wrong, cut into old wood or miss the trim two years running, and you end up with an open, woody plant you cannot recover by cutting. Burncoose Nurseries, the specialist nursery at Caerhays Estate in Cornwall, have filmed a practical demonstration of pruning French lavender that lays out exactly where the cutting line should go. This article takes that demonstration and adds what it cannot include: which lavender species and cultivars actually suit the chalk slopes above Bridge and the frost-prone Nailbourne valley floor, and where the nesting season creates a timing conflict in the late-summer deadhead window.

Watch first: Pruning French Lavender (Burncoose Nurseries)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=E0A1_yWC7IE  ·  Channel: Burncoose Nurseries

What the video shows

Burncoose Nurseries is part of Caerhays Estate in Cornwall and has been producing practical plant-care videos for many years. Their French lavender pruning guide demonstrates the technique on Lavandula stoechas, the tufted, rabbit-eared species that flowers from late spring through summer. The key points the video covers are:

  • When to prune French lavender. Early in the video the timing is set out: French lavender is best pruned after the main flowering flush is spent, which in a normal UK spring falls from late May into June. Waiting until the flowers have fully finished avoids cutting off buds that have not yet opened. A second lighter tidy is shown as appropriate after any late-summer rebloom.
  • Where to make the cut. The demonstration places the cut above the old wood, into the soft, green current-season growth, keeping a cushion of leafy stems above the lignified base. Cutting into the hard, brown, woody stems below the green is shown as the error to avoid: French lavender, like its English cousin, will not reliably regenerate from old wood, and a cut made too low into the base leaves a dead stump.
  • How much to remove. Toward the middle of the video the presenter shows that removing roughly a third of the season's growth, taking the plant back to a neat rounded shape but retaining plenty of fresh foliage, keeps the plant compact over successive years without stressing it. The goal is to tighten the mound, not reduce the overall height dramatically.
  • Tool choice. Hand shears are used throughout the demonstration rather than powered trimmers. For a low border hedge or a specimen the manual approach gives better control over the cutting height and reduces the risk of catching old wood by accident.
  • Feeding and aftercare. Toward the end of the video a brief note is made that lavender does not need rich feeding: too much nitrogen produces soft, floppy growth that opens up the mound. On chalk or sandy soil the plant feeds itself adequately from the lean mineral ground.

The video is focused on French lavender (Lavandula stoechas) specifically. The same principles apply broadly to English lavender (Lavandula angustifolia), though the timing of the main trim shifts slightly later in the year for English lavender, which typically flowers from late June into July.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

French versus English lavender for North Downs hedging. The video demonstrates French lavender, which is showier in flower with its distinctive pineapple-shaped heads, but it is meaningfully less hardy than English lavender. In a sheltered garden on the chalk slopes above Bridge, French lavender (Lavandula stoechas) will often come through a typical Kent winter without serious damage. But in a hard year, with sustained frost below minus eight or nine degrees, it suffers dieback that English lavender would shrug off. For a formal low hedge intended to last ten years or more on the chalk above Bridge, I recommend English lavender cultivars: 'Hidcote' (compact, deep violet, one of the most reliable for hedging), 'Munstead' (slightly shorter, lavender-blue, very free-flowering), or 'Vera' (larger habit, good for a taller edging hedge). These are all Lavandula angustifolia selections that have proved themselves in UK conditions over many decades. For the chalk planting conditions around Bridge, any of the three will establish well in the free-draining, alkaline ground.

Why chalk suits lavender so well. The North Downs chalk that runs across the slopes between Bridge village and the lanes to Patrixbourne and Bekesbourne is genuinely excellent lavender ground. Lavender is a Mediterranean plant adapted to thin, alkaline, well-drained soil and does not want to sit in wet over winter. On the chalk slopes above Bridge, where drainage is fast and the pH is on the alkaline side, the conditions match what the plant wants. This is one of the reasons lavender hedges are a practical choice here that would not suit heavier, wetter soils on the valley clay further north. The lean mineral chalk also discourages the overly lush, floppy growth that a rich, fertilised soil produces.

The Nailbourne valley floor is a different proposition. The valley bottom around Bridge, Bishopsbourne and Barham retains moisture and channels cold air on still nights to produce genuine frost pockets. French lavender in particular is not a good choice for these conditions: the combination of heavier soil, winter waterlogging where the chalk gives way to valley alluvium or clay, and hard overnight frosts in cold years makes it unreliable. Even English lavender on the valley floor requires very sharp drainage and is better placed on a raised bed or against a south-facing wall where air drainage is better. On valley-floor sites where a client wants a low aromatic border hedge, rosemary on a well-drained raised bed is a more considered choice than either lavender species. If the requirement is a low flowering evergreen rather than an aromatic one, the small-leaved hardy hebes are worth considering for a sheltered valley-floor position; the hebe pruning guide covers which cultivars are reliably hardy in a Nailbourne frost pocket.

The nesting season and the summer trim window. Lavender's ideal trim timing, after the main flowering flush, falls squarely inside the nesting season. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest of any wild bird. The nesting season runs from 1 March through to the end of August, and some late broods, particularly wrens and house sparrows, extend well into September. Lavender grown as a formal edging hedge or low mound along a sunny border path is exactly the kind of dense, ground-level vegetation that wrens and dunnocks use for late nesting. Before any trim in May, June, July or August, I work slowly along the hedge, parting the growth at intervals and stopping if I find anything active. On a lavender hedge that has not been trimmed yet this season, the safest window is September, after most late broods are out. The resulting cut is slightly woodier than a late-May trim would produce, but it is the legal and ecologically sound approach.

The no-old-wood rule and the discipline it requires. The video makes this point clearly, but it is worth emphasising for the Bridge area where I often see lavender hedges that have been left for three or four years without a trim. Once lavender has been allowed to extend long, bare, woody stems at the base with only foliage at the tips, the annual-trim approach can no longer restore it: cutting back to where the foliage is means cutting into the old wood below, and the plant will not regenerate from there. The only recovery at that stage is to remove the plants and replant with young stock. This is the same hard constraint that applies to rosemary: both are Mediterranean sub-shrubs that need annual maintenance to stay hedge-like, and skipping it for two or three seasons crosses a point of no return.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area and formal lavender borders. Lavender hedges and borders within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area are almost always in a routine maintenance category rather than a structural-works category. The six-week prior-notification requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 applies to trees and hedges with stems at or above 75mm diameter at 1.5m height. An established lavender hedge, even an old one, very rarely reaches this threshold: the individual stems are slender, and the plants are typically removed and replanted before they could. In practice, a straight lavender trim within the CA requires no prior notification. If a client in the CA has what appears to be a very old specimen with heavily lignified, thickened main stems, I would still check the stem diameter before any significant cutting, but this is exceptional. The conservation-area notice guide covers the general process for any work near the Bifrons Park boundary.
