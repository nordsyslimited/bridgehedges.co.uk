Hard pruning a laurel screen: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Cherry laurel grows fast enough that a screen left alone for four or five years can double its depth and add a metre or more in height. Burncoose Nurseries, a long-established Cornish nursery with genuine horticultural expertise, show the right technique for pulling it back without killing it. This guide takes their demonstration and adds what it cannot cover: the nesting-law timing, the conservation-area checks and the Nailbourne-valley detail that change how the job sits on this ground specifically.

Watch first: Hard Pruning Laurel hedges, Burncoose Nurseries

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=Znfmy1gtH0c (Burncoose Nurseries)

What the video shows

Burncoose Nurseries is a family-run nursery in Cornwall with a plant-trial and propagation background that gives their pruning advice genuine practical grounding. This video is one of a short series on laurel management, covering the point at which routine annual trimming is no longer enough. It demonstrates:

  • Why cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) tolerates hard pruning that would kill most other evergreen screen plants. Unlike Leylandii or other true conifers, laurel regenerates freely from old, bare wood, and a screen cut back to stumps will typically reshoot within a single growing season.
  • The case for loppers and hand-pruning rather than a powered hedge trimmer when removing significant amounts of the canopy. Because laurel leaves are large, a machine cuts through the leaf blades and the cut edges brown back noticeably. Individual branch cuts made just above a node or healthy bud look clean and recover faster.
  • The appropriate cutting level for a hard prune: the video shows reducing stems to 30-60 cm above the ground in extreme cases, but the principle early in the video is working in stages, taking the top back by roughly a third in year one and the sides the following winter, to avoid stressing the plant in one pass.
  • What to expect from the regrowth: a brief bare period through late winter before new shoots emerge from the remaining stems. The new growth comes in soft and fast; the first season after a hard prune usually shows a metre or more of new shoots by late summer.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The timing window is not optional. A hard prune of any evergreen screen is a substantial physical intervention, and on laurel it means removing large sections of canopy. That makes the nesting-law check more important than it is on a light annual trim. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The practical window for a hard prune is November through to the end of February, well inside the dormant period and well clear of the nesting season (1 March to 31 August). I would not do a hard laurel reduction between March and August without a careful nest check immediately before starting, and even then I'd hold back if there was any active nesting in the canopy. The nesting-law and cutting-timing guide on this site covers the legal baseline in full.

The Nailbourne valley floor is damper than the chalk slopes above. Cherry laurel is not drought-sensitive in the way some natives are, but it grows faster where moisture is reliable. Screens on the valley bottom towards Bekesbourne and Patrixbourne typically need intervention sooner than a comparable hedge on the chalk above the village. In practice I'd say a valley-floor laurel screen that hasn't been touched in five years will be harder work than the Burncoose video implies, because the growth is denser and the woody stems thicker. It is worth being realistic about what a staged reduction involves before starting.

Stem thickness and the conservation-area notice. Cherry laurel grows stems quickly and a screen that has been in place for ten years or more can have main stems well over 75mm diameter at 1.5m height. If the hedge falls within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, any works to stems at or above that threshold require six weeks' prior notice to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. I check the address against the CA boundary before quoting for this kind of work, not afterwards. If notice is needed, the timing can be incorporated into the winter schedule without difficulty.

Laurel screens and neighbour disputes. Cherry laurel is one of the commonest plants cited in High Hedge complaints in Kent, because a well-watered screen can genuinely grow to a problematic height in a short time. If the impetus for the hard prune is a dispute over light rather than straightforward maintenance, the relevant legislation is Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, which deals with high hedges over 2m of evergreen or semi-evergreen growth. Canterbury City Council administers the process for the Bridge parish. A hard prune to a manageable height resolves the practical problem whether or not a formal complaint has been made, but it is worth knowing the legal route is there if negotiation stalls.

If the screen is beyond recovery. Occasionally a hard prune that goes into very old, woody stems on a neglected screen will not regenerate evenly. Gaps can stay bare for more than one season, and a screen with significant dieback may not regain its density. If the prune reveals that the structure is too far gone, it is worth thinking about removal and replanting with a species better suited to the ground. On the chalk above Bridge, hornbeam is a good formal alternative; on the damper valley floor, native field-maple and hawthorn mixes will establish well and are less likely to outgrow control. The guide to planting a bare-root hedge on North Downs chalk covers the species options and soil preparation in detail.
