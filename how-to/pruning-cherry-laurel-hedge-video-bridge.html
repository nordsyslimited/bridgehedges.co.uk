Annual maintenance trim for a cherry laurel hedge: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Cherry laurel is probably the most common evergreen boundary hedge in the villages around Bridge, and the reason it ends up needing renovation cuts every few years is usually that annual maintenance gets skipped. Burncoose Nurseries, a long-established Cornish nursery, show the right approach to the annual trim. This guide takes their demonstration and adds the timing, tool-choice and Nailbourne-valley context that changes how the job works on this ground specifically.

Watch first: Pruning a laurel hedge, Burncoose Nurseries

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=VK4uNNDCrco (Burncoose Nurseries)

What the video shows

Burncoose Nurseries is a family-run Cornish nursery with a plant-trial and propagation background. Their short pruning guides are practical demonstrations rather than classroom theory. This video covers maintenance trimming of an established cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) hedge rather than the hard renovation work they demonstrate elsewhere. It addresses:

  • Why an annual maintenance cut is a straightforward job when done on time, and how skipping it for two or three seasons is what turns a manageable hedge into a renovation project. The Burncoose position is clear: trim at least once a year, and the hedge stays under control.
  • The leaf-size problem and why the tool choice matters for laurel specifically. Cherry laurel leaves are large and thick, and a hedge trimmer or powered cutter that slices through the blade leaves brown, desiccated cut edges that persist for weeks. The video makes the case for hand tools: secateurs or a single-bladed blade rather than a machine with opposing reciprocating blades, so cuts land at or just above a node rather than bisecting a leaf.
  • How much to take off in a routine maintenance cut: removing the current season's growth to keep the hedge within its established footprint, without cutting back into the hard, older wood that would be needed for renovation. Keeping the hedge slightly tapered (narrower at the top) means the lower growth stays in light and remains dense.
  • What recovery looks like after a clean annual trim: a short flush of pale new shoots follows within weeks of a well-timed cut, giving the hedge a fresh, even face before winter.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Burncoose's video is a general nursery demonstration produced in Cornwall, so here is what changes when the hedge is in Bridge or the Nailbourne valley villages.

Timing and the nesting season. The timing that matters most around Bridge is not horticultural, it is legal. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. The nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August as a practical guide, and cherry laurel hedges are among the most popular nesting habitats in domestic gardens in this part of Kent: they are dense, dark, and impenetrable, which is exactly what nesting birds are looking for. I always check for active nests before any cut during that window, and in most cases I recommend scheduling the annual laurel trim for September, once the season has closed and the hedge has finished most of its growth flush.

Growth rate in the Nailbourne valley. Cherry laurel grows noticeably faster on the fertile valley-floor ground close to the Nailbourne than it does on the chalk slopes above Bridge village. The valley bottom sits on a mixture of alluvial material and clay-with-flints rather than pure chalk, and it holds more moisture through dry spells. A hedge that puts on 30 to 40 cm of new growth per season on the chalk may put on 50 to 60 cm or more in the valley bottom. That does not change the approach in the video, but it does mean that a hedge left for two years in the valley needs more to come off than the video's general guidance might suggest. If I quote for an annual maintenance contract on a valley-floor property, I factor in the faster growth rate when calculating time.

Tool choice on a larger hedge. The Burncoose video covers a relatively modest domestic hedge. For a taller laurel boundary, say 2 m or above, the case for hand tools and a step ladder or platform is stronger still. I use secateurs for the top face and loppers for any stems that have thickened beyond what secateurs will close cleanly on. For hedges that are genuinely too big to do by hand in a reasonable time, a single-blade hedge trimmer gives a cleaner result than a reciprocating-blade machine, and the browning is less severe if the cut is made just above a bud rather than midleaf. The hard-pruning guide covers what to do when a screen has already been left too long and the annual approach is no longer enough. If the large-leaf constraint is the main reason for hesitating over cherry laurel, it is worth knowing that Portugal laurel (Prunus lusitanica) tolerates a power hedgetrimmer cleanly and offers a similar dense, evergreen boundary on the same chalk soils.

Leaf toxicity and arisings. Cherry laurel leaves and berries contain compounds that break down to produce prussic acid, and the arisings are toxic to horses and cattle. Around the farms and smallholdings towards Lower Hardres and Bishopsbourne I bag all laurel clippings and take them off site rather than leaving them in a heap or adding them to a compost pile that animals might access. It is worth confirming this with the property owner before the cut.

Conservation-area check. A mature cherry laurel near the Bifrons Park Conservation Area boundary can easily exceed the 75 mm stem-diameter threshold that triggers the notice requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Routine annual trimming of established topgrowth on a hedge does not generally require notice, but where I am reducing height or taking off significant side material on a specimen inside the CA, I confirm with Canterbury City Council whether a six-week notice is needed first. The conservation area notice guide covers the process in full.
