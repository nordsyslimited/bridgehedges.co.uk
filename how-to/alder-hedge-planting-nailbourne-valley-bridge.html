Planting an alder hedge on the Nailbourne valley floor: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Alder (Alnus glutinosa) is the species I would suggest for anyone in the lower Nailbourne valley who needs a native boundary hedge on ground that gets seasonally wet. It is, in one sense, the mirror image of copper beech: where beech wants free-draining chalk slopes, alder is at home in the heavy, damp soil that sits in the valley between Bridge and Bishopsbourne. Heathwood Nurseries, UK specialists in British seed-grown native plants, have a clear short film on getting alder correctly into the ground. What it cannot address is the specific case for this winterbourne valley, the wildlife return that alder delivers here in particular, and the nesting-season and conservation-area questions that apply in the Bridge parish.

Watch first: How to plant an Alder Tree – Heathwood Nurseries

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=fEybQ5_b_5I  ·  Channel: Heathwood Nurseries

What the video shows

Wayne at Heathwood Nurseries demonstrates the practical steps for getting an alder into the ground, using the nursery's British seed-grown cell-grown stock as the example. The video is titled as a tree planting guide, but the technique is directly applicable to establishing alder as a boundary hedge: the same stock types, spacings and initial pruning approach apply whether you are planting a single specimen or a continuous hedge run along a damp boundary. The key points covered:

  • Stock type and timing. Heathwood stock is cell-grown from British seed, which gives it a compact fibrous root system that establishes well without the bare-root dormancy constraints. That said, the best planting window for any alder hedge is the dormant season: late October through to the end of February, when the plant is not in active growth and soil moisture is high. Early November is particularly good in the Nailbourne valley, when the ground is workable but before the worst of the winter waterlogging begins.
  • Site preparation. The video shows clearing competing vegetation from the planting strip before the roots go in. For an alder hedge along a field boundary or garden edge in the valley, this means removing coarse grass, nettles and any bramble. A spade-wide trench rather than individual holes works better for a continuous hedge line, and encourages roots to spread along the run from the start.
  • Planting depth and spacing. Alder goes in at the nursery soil mark: the root collar should sit level with the surrounding ground, not below it. The video is clear on this. For a dense informal boundary hedge, plants spaced at around 45 centimetres apart will knit together within two or three growing seasons. For a more open, wildlife-friendly structure, 60 to 75 centimetres works well and still produces a recognisable hedge line.
  • Firming in and immediate aftercare. Firming in after backfilling removes air pockets that can dry out the root ball during establishment, even on wet sites. The video emphasises watering at planting: even in a damp valley, newly planted stock can dry at the root ball if the surrounding soil has not yet settled against it.
  • Initial pruning at planting. As with other native hedging species, cutting the newly planted stems back by roughly a third at planting stimulates branching from low down. This is the step most people skip, because it feels wrong to reduce something just put in. It is the right call: alder left uncut will tend to produce one or two dominant leaders and a sparse open base for the first few years, rather than the dense growth that makes a good hedge.

The video does not cover the seasonally wet soil conditions of the Nailbourne valley, the wildlife value of alder in this specific setting, or the legal and conservation-area questions that apply in Bridge parish. Those are the parts worth reading below.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Why the valley floor suits alder. The Nailbourne is a winterbourne: a chalk-fed stream that flows seasonally, typically from late autumn through spring, then dries out through summer in most years. The valley floor between Bridge and Bishopsbourne carries heavier clay-over-chalk soil than the slopes above, and in the wet months it can be waterlogged for sustained periods. Most hedging species perform poorly in these conditions: beech, hornbeam and many ornamental choices lose vigour or thin at the base when roots sit in standing water through winter. Alnus glutinosa is the opposite. It is a native riparian species that evolved alongside seasonally flooded river margins, and it handles prolonged waterlogging without complaint. On damp valley-floor boundaries near the Nailbourne, or along the wetter edges of fields towards Bekesbourne and Bishopsbourne, alder is not just a viable choice: it is probably the most site-appropriate native hedging species available.

Why the chalk slopes above Bridge are the wrong ground for alder. Alder's preference for moisture is also its limitation. The thin, freely draining, highly alkaline chalk soils on the slopes above Bridge and along the lanes towards Lower Hardres and Barham are too dry in summer for alder to establish well and remain vigorous. On that ground, native species such as hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, beech or field maple are better choices, and the bare-root hedge planting guide covers the species-to-soil matching for the chalk slope sites in more detail. Alder belongs in the valley, not on the hill.

Nitrogen fixation and soil improvement. One practical benefit of alder that is rarely mentioned in planting guides is its nitrogen-fixing root nodules. Like legumes, alder forms a symbiosis with the soil bacterium Frankia alni, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen directly into the root zone. Over several years, an established alder hedge improves the nutrient content of the surrounding soil, which benefits neighbouring planting and the grass sward on either side of the boundary. On the lower valley floor, where the clay-over-chalk soil can be nutrient-poor under long-established grass, this is a genuine additional return from choosing alder over a non-fixing species.

Wildlife value in this setting. Alder delivers some of the earliest wildlife interest of any native hedging species. The male catkins open in late January or February, providing pollen at a time when very little else is available to early bees and other insects. The woody seed cones (technically strobiles, but most people call them cones) ripen in autumn and persist on the tree through winter, and they are a reliable food source for siskins and lesser redpolls, two small finches that move through the Nailbourne valley during the colder months. The leaf litter from alder is particularly rich in invertebrate life compared with many other native species, which feeds the food chain from the ground up. For anyone planting a hedge with genuine wildlife benefit in mind, alder on wet valley-floor ground is one of the best native choices available locally. See also the mixed wildlife hedge planting guide for how alder fits into a multi-species native hedge mix alongside hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple.

Nesting season and the planting window. The dormant-season planting window (October to February) sits well clear of the main nesting season under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which runs from 1 March to the end of August. The initial formative cut at planting, and any early pruning in the first winter, presents no nesting risk at all. The first maintenance cut on an established alder hedge, typically in the second or third year, does fall in the period when hedgerow birds use the hedge: I check for active nests before any trimming work between 1 March and 31 August, and I would not cut during that window without checking first.

Countryside Stewardship and hedgerow gapping-up. If the purpose is filling a gap in an existing field-boundary hedgerow rather than establishing a new hedge from scratch, the Countryside Stewardship hedgerow gapping-up grant (capital item BN7) may apply. This covers native species planting to fill gaps of up to 20 metres in established countryside hedgerows, and alder is eligible for wet boundary sites within the qualifying criteria. The scheme is administered through the Rural Payments Agency: check the gov.uk Countryside Stewardship guidance for current payment rates and eligibility conditions, as these change between scheme years and I cannot give a reliable current figure here.

Conservation-area check for mature alder. An alder planted as a hedge today will, if allowed to grow without regular management, develop into a multi-stem tree with stems well above the 75mm diameter threshold relevant to conservation-area work. Any cutting, uprooting or wilful damage to a tree within a conservation area requires six weeks prior notification to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 once stems reach that size. The Bifrons Park Conservation Area covers land around Patrixbourne and parts of the Bridge parish. Established or self-seeded alder trees near the Nailbourne in that area may already be at or above this threshold. I check stem diameters and the CA boundary before any structural work on mature alder near the conservation-area boundary. The conservation-area notice guide explains the notification process in full.
