Healthy hedgerow management: cutting frequency, wildlife and the PTES lifecycle guide for Bridge, Kent.

Most hedges around Bridge get cut too often and always to exactly the same line. The result is a hedge that looks maintained but is steadily dying inside: a solid collar of deadwood just below the cut, no blossom in spring, no berries in autumn, and gaps forming at the base where light has not reached in years. The People's Trust for Endangered Species (PTES) has been surveying and advising on UK hedgerow health for many years, and their February 2025 short film "Management for Healthy Hedgerows" is the clearest explanation I have come across of what actually goes wrong when a hedge is managed the way most people manage theirs, and what a different approach produces.

Watch first: Management for Healthy Hedgerows (Tom Hartwell / PTES)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=_UV3cUE5QDc  ·  Featuring: PTES (People's Trust for Endangered Species)

What the video shows

PTES runs the Great British Hedgerow Survey and publishes the Healthy Hedgerows management guidance used by farmers, landowners and conservation bodies across England. Megan Gimber, PTES Key Habitats Officer, presents the main management principles in straightforward, practical terms. The film is six minutes long and covers:

  • The scar-tissue problem. When a hedge is cut to exactly the same height and width year after year, deadwood builds up just inside the cut line. The hedge appears solid from outside, but the structure below the scar is increasingly moribund. Eventually the hedge starts to thin at the base as light fails to reach the lower stems, and the characteristic dense, berry-rich profile that makes a mature hedgerow valuable to wildlife is lost. Megan Gimber describes this as one of the most common and least visible forms of hedgerow damage in England, because the hedge looks managed, so the problem is invisible until it is quite advanced.
  • Incremental trimming: the alternative. Rather than cutting to the same line every time, the film recommends going approximately 10cm higher and wider than the previous cut. Over several years, this lets the hedge slowly expand into a wider, more structurally complex form with live wood throughout, while still being manageable. The technique keeps live growth ahead of the cut line so that the scar never gets a chance to form into a persistent deadwood collar.
  • Cutting frequency: the two-to-three year principle. PTES guidance emphasises that cutting once every two years, and preferably once every three years, produces dramatically more blossom and berry than an annual cut. The reason is biological: flowers and berries on most hedgerow species (hawthorn, blackthorn, elder, dog rose, field maple) are produced on two-year-old and older wood. An annual cut removes that wood before it can flower or set fruit. For a hedgerow managed partly for wildlife, cutting every other year at a minimum should be the default. This is the single most impactful change most hedgerow owners can make.
  • Rejuvenation: coppicing and laying. Where a hedge has already been over-managed for many years and has developed a dense deadwood collar or significant gaps at the base, the film covers two rejuvenation options. Coppicing (cutting individual stems back to a low stump) stimulates regrowth from the base and can rebuild a hedge that has thinned out. Hedge laying is the more intensive option, described in the film as the gold standard for creating a dense, stockproof, structurally rich hedgerow from stems that have been allowed to grow on. Laying is covered in depth in the guide to traditional hedge laying for Bridge and the Nailbourne valley.
  • Incorporating tall trees. Allowing selected hedgerow stems to grow up as standard trees, rather than coppicing or trimming them back with the rest of the hedge, significantly increases the structural diversity and wildlife value of the hedgerow corridor. The film recommends identifying one or two suitable stems per 100 metres of hedge and leaving them uncut. For the Nailbourne valley, the native species most likely to be found as natural hedgerow trees are ash, field maple, hawthorn (occasionally grown to tree form), hazel multi-stems and, on the chalk slopes, wayfaring tree.

The film does not address the legal position for cutting timing, the difference between garden and agricultural hedgerows in terms of regulatory coverage, or the specific species mix and soil conditions of the North Downs chalk and Nailbourne valley floor. Those are covered below.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Two separate legal frameworks apply to hedge cutting timing in this area, and both point to the same window. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. In practice, this means that any cutting work in a hedge that could disturb an active nest during the main nesting season (1 March to 31 August) carries legal risk. For the full detail on how this applies to garden hedges and service schedules, see the guide to hedge cutting timing and the nesting law.

The second framework applies specifically to field boundary hedgerows on or adjacent to agricultural land. The Management of Hedgerows (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/680), which came into force on 23 May 2024, prohibit cutting, trimming, uprooting or otherwise destroying any hedgerow on or next to agricultural land in England between 1 March and 31 August. This reinstates protections that had been lost and applies to any contractor or landowner working on a field boundary hedge in the Nailbourne valley, the valley slopes around Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne, Bishopsbourne, Kingston and Barham, or the chalk downland hedges running up towards Lower Hardres. The cut window for agricultural hedgerows is therefore 1 September to the end of February, the same window that works for the nesting law. I do all field boundary hedge work in this area from early September through to late February, and I would advise any landowner managing their own hedges to do the same.

The North Downs chalk slopes and the Nailbourne valley floor behave differently. The hedgerow species mix around Bridge reflects the dual character of the land. On the chalk slopes rising towards Lower Hardres and Patrixbourne, the dominant species are hawthorn, blackthorn, wayfaring tree (Viburnum lantana), field maple and dog rose, with occasional spindle and elder. These are well-adapted to the free-draining, alkaline chalk and can go two or three years between cuts without producing unmanageable growth. On the heavier, moister valley floor around the Nailbourne stream, particularly around Bishopsbourne and Bekesbourne, hazel, elder and dog rose grow notably faster. A valley-floor hedgerow managed on a two-year cut cycle will put on more growth per cycle than its chalk-slope equivalent. This is not a reason to cut more frequently; it is a reason to plan the incremental-trimming line carefully so the hedge has room to expand without overrunning the boundary or the lane verge. The wildlife value of a well-managed valley-floor hedgerow is high: the Nailbourne is a chalk winterbourne with the invertebrate and bird diversity associated with a relatively undisturbed river valley, and the hedgerow network feeding into it is part of that.

The Bifrons Park Conservation Area and older hedgerow trees. Any hedgerow stems within or on the boundary of the Bifrons Park Conservation Area that have been allowed to grow into standard trees can reach the 75mm stem-diameter threshold that triggers a six-week notice requirement to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Routine cutting of a maintained hedgerow does not raise this issue, but removing a naturally grown hedgerow tree, coppicing a large multi-stemmed hazel that has grown well above hedge height, or laying a stem of that size is a different matter. I check the address against the CA boundary before any structural work of that kind. For the full process on conservation area notices, see the guide to conservation area tree and hedge notices for Bridge.

What I see on the ground in Bridge. The hedges I am asked to maintain in this area fall into two broad categories. The first is well-managed native hedgerows: hawthorn and blackthorn dominated, often with a good showing of elder and dog rose, berry-rich in autumn, with a mix of stem ages. These are usually on holdings where the landowner or previous occupier has taken a light-touch approach, cutting every other year or less often. The second is over-managed garden-boundary hedges, particularly privet, Leylandii and laurel in village gardens: cut hard to the same line every spring and sometimes again in summer, narrow at the top, dead inside, and carrying very little wildlife value. The PTES film makes the case for the first approach clearly and without being preachy about it. For most of the hedges I look after around Bridge, moving from an annual to a biennial cut, and letting the cut line creep outward by 10cm each time, would make a noticeable difference to the berry crop within two or three seasons. It is not a dramatic intervention; it is just a change in the schedule. For the contractor technique side of making that change on a working farm boundary, the UKCEH and NAAC guide to sympathetic hedge cutting covers the practical approach.
