Bay laurel hedge and topiary pruning: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis) is the culinary bay: aromatic, non-toxic, entirely different from the cherry laurel that lines most boundaries in this area. It appears increasingly in formal SE England gardens as a clipped hedge, as a standard (the classic lollipop shape), or as a loose informal screen. Lee Bestall, a UK garden professional presenting for Spear & Jackson, produced a practical pruning guide covering all three forms. This guide takes his demonstration and adds the frost-pocket timing, chalk-soil context and local constraints that the video cannot cover from a distance.

Watch first: Pruning bay trees with Lee Bestall (Spear & Jackson)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=9oLCWE_FatM (Spear & Jackson, presented by Lee Bestall)

What the video shows

Spear & Jackson is a Sheffield garden tool company founded in 1760. Their “This Month In The Garden” series brings in Lee Bestall, a UK garden professional, to demonstrate practical pruning work across different plant types. This guide covers bay laurel pruning across its main garden forms: hedge, free-standing shrub and topiary standard. It addresses:

  • Why secateurs give a cleaner result than a hedge trimmer on bay laurel. Bay laurel leaves are large, leathery and aromatic. A reciprocating hedge trimmer cuts through the leaf mid-blade, leaving brown, desiccated edges that persist for several weeks and make the plant look untidy. Secateurs, cutting cleanly above a node or at the junction with a lateral, avoid this entirely. The video makes this point clearly, and it is the most practically useful piece of guidance for anyone who has been using a powered trimmer on a bay hedge and wondering why the result looks worse each year.
  • When to prune: late spring, after the frost risk has passed. The main pruning window for bay laurel is late May or early June for most of the UK. The reason for waiting is that new growth after a prune is soft and frost-susceptible. Pruning in early spring and catching a late frost on fresh growth sets the plant back significantly and creates the same frost-damaged appearance that the video goes on to explain how to correct.
  • Identifying and removing frost-damaged wood. Bay laurel leaves and stems blacken and die back after a sharp frost. The video demonstrates the scratch test: pressing a fingernail lightly through the bark of a suspect stem. Green, faintly moist cambium beneath the bark means the stem is alive; pale brown or dry white tissue means it is dead. The cut is made back to live growth, just above a healthy bud or lateral. This is a genuinely common task on bay in SE England after a cold winter and the guidance in the video is straightforward and practical.
  • Maintaining a standard (lollipop) form. For bay grown as a standard, the video demonstrates working round the ball head systematically with secateurs, aiming for an even dome. It also covers removing side shoots that break from the main clear stem, which is an ongoing task on standards: bay puts out growth readily from the stem if the plant is growing strongly, and these shoots need removing before they thicken.
  • Hedge and informal screen pruning. For bay grown as a hedge or trained as a loose screen, the same secateur approach applies. The video notes that keeping cuts just above a node produces a quicker flush of new growth to cover the cut surface. For a formal hedge with a clean face, working in sections with a taut string line gives a better result than freehand.

The video does not address the frost-pocket conditions of the Nailbourne valley floor, the distinction from the laurel species more commonly seen in this area, or the conservation-area and nesting-season constraints that affect timing around Bridge. Those are the parts worth reading further.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Bay laurel is not cherry laurel. This matters more here than in most parts of the country because cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) is so common in the Nailbourne valley villages that "laurel" means cherry laurel by default in most conversations I have on site. Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis) is a completely different plant: Mediterranean in origin, culinary, aromatic and not related to the Prunus family at all. Its leaves are not toxic in the way cherry laurel's leaves and berries are, and it does not share cherry laurel's very vigorous habit. Portugal laurel (Prunus lusitanica) is different again: a Prunus like cherry laurel, with smaller leaves that suit a hedge trimmer, used as a formal boundary hedge rather than a culinary plant. The quickest way to identify Laurus nobilis is to crush a leaf: the spicy, distinctly aromatic smell is unmistakeable.

Chalk soils and the North Downs. Bay laurel is well suited to the alkaline, free-draining chalk of the North Downs above Bridge and along the lanes towards Patrixbourne, Bishopsbourne and Lower Hardres. It is a Mediterranean species from limestone hillside habitats, and tolerating alkaline conditions is part of its native range. I have seen established bay hedges on the chalk slopes around Bridge looking thoroughly at home: clean, dense foliage and reliable annual growth. The main establishment risk is dry summers in the first two to three years before the root system has found enough depth to buffer a drought. Irrigation in the first summer after planting is worthwhile on the thinner chalk above 100m, where the top few centimetres of soil can dry out within days of a dry spell in June or July.

The frost-pocket problem on the Nailbourne valley floor. Bay laurel is reliably hardy to around -5°C for established plants, but it suffers dieback in colder winters and young plants under three years old can be killed outright by a hard frost. The Nailbourne valley floor, particularly the sections around Bishopsbourne and Barham, sits in a cold-air drainage frost pocket: air chills on the North Downs slopes above Bridge and Patrixbourne and runs down to settle on the valley floor, arriving noticeably earlier and harder than on the slopes above. This is the most significant constraint on bay laurel in this specific area. If I am planting bay on a low-lying valley-floor property, I note the frost-pocket risk and recommend fleece protection for young plants in the first two or three winters. For established plants, the guidance in the video applies: cut back to live growth in spring using the scratch test, and defer the main pruning until late May to avoid exposing new growth to a cold snap.

Why late May is later than the general guidance suggests. Most UK gardening advice gives "after the last frost" as the trigger for the main bay pruning. Around Bridge, that general guidance needs some caution. The Nailbourne valley floor can receive air frosts well into May in a cold spring, and the chalk slopes above Bridge are exposed to north and east winds that make late frosts sharper than in a sheltered garden further south. I trim bay hedges and standards here from late May onwards, not from April, and I encourage customers to do the same. The light second tidy in late summer that the video mentions, to keep the shape crisp through winter, works well in September here: early enough to let the plant settle before the first frosts, late enough that any nesting birds have finished.

The nesting season and bay laurel. Bay laurel in its looser, less clipped forms, particularly a specimen that has been left uncut for a season or more and has built up dense interior growth, can support nesting birds. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. The active nesting period runs from 1 March to 31 August, and I always check for active nests before cutting, particularly on established, overgrown bay specimens where dense growth has built up inside the canopy. Bay kept as a neatly clipped annual hedge is less likely to harbour active nests than a loose, neglected specimen, but the check takes two minutes. The nesting-season guide covers the legal position in full.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area. An established bay standard or a bay hedge left for several years without trimming can produce stems and trunks that exceed 75mm diameter at ground level, which triggers the six-week notice requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area. Routine annual clipping of a bay hedge or topiary standard does not generally reach this threshold, but removal of an established specimen or a hard renovation cut on a large bay screen inside the CA warrants a check with Canterbury City Council before starting work. The conservation-area notice guide covers the process in full.
