How to trim hedges, and when: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Most general hedge-trimming videos get the technique right and leave the legal timing to a passing mention, if that. This guide takes a genuinely useful all-round trimming video and fills the gap: exactly what the nesting law requires, and how the North Downs and the Nailbourne valley change the calendar around Bridge.

Watch first: How to cut & trim hedges, the ultimate guide for perfect garden hedges

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=_Dc3B2I7bu4

What the video shows

A general-purpose guide to trimming garden hedges, useful groundwork before any of the species-specific videos elsewhere in these guides. It covers:

  • Choosing the right tool for hedge size and stem thickness, hand shears versus a powered trimmer versus loppers for thicker growth.
  • Using a string line or cane as a straight-edge guide for a level top.
  • Battering the sides (narrower at the top, wider at the base) so lower growth still gets light and the hedge stays dense from top to bottom.
  • Working in sections on a long hedge rather than trying to finish one continuous pass, to keep the line consistent.

What the video does not go into in any depth is exactly when, in the calendar, any of this is legally allowed. That is the part worth getting right.

What it doesn't cover: the nesting law and this ground specifically

The legal baseline, everywhere in the UK. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is being built or in use, or to intentionally kill, injure or take a wild bird, or its eggs. There is no fixed calendar date written into the Act itself, but in practice active nests are in place from 1 March through to the end of August, with the RSPB and DEFRA both treating that window as the core nesting period to avoid. Late broods can run into September. The safest default, and the one I work to, is to hold anything beyond a light trim until 1 September, and to plan hard reductions or hedge-laying for the dormant window, November through to the end of February.

Checking before you cut, not after. A visual check for active nests before starting, every time, on every hedge, is the difference between a defensible position and a genuine offence. Thick, dense hedges, the kind that make the best garden boundary, are exactly the kind birds prefer, so this is not a box-ticking exercise.

What changes around Bridge. None of the legal timing above is Bridge-specific, it is national law. What is local is the species mix it applies to: hawthorn and blackthorn field boundaries towards Patrixbourne and Lower Hardres carry nests earlier in spring than some ornamental evergreens, so the pre-March window closes for those hedges first. Anything close to Bifrons Park also needs the section-211 stem check (75mm at 1.5m, six weeks' notice to Canterbury City Council) done alongside the nesting check, not as a separate afterthought.

High hedges, separately. If the issue is a neighbour's evergreen screen blocking light rather than routine timing, that sits under Part 8 of the Anti-Social Behaviour Act 2003 instead, a different process, with Canterbury City Council as the administering authority for the Bridge parish.
