Yew hedge pruning: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Yew is native to the North Downs chalk and it shows, mature yew hedging around Bridge and Bishopsbourne holds a sharp line for decades with a single annual cut. This guide works through a real nursery pruning demonstration, then covers the chalk-slope detail that makes yew such a natural fit for this ground specifically.

Watch first: Pruning a yew / Taxus hedge, Burncoose Nurseries

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=Cob1ZRm-aR8 (Burncoose Nurseries)

What the video shows

Burncoose Nurseries, a working Cornish nursery with genuine horticultural expertise in Taxus, demonstrate pruning a yew hedge. The video covers:

  • Why yew tolerates hard pruning better than almost any other conifer, it will reshoot from old, leafless wood where most conifers will not.
  • The recommended annual timing, September or October in the UK, once the year's growth has hardened off.
  • Keeping cut faces slightly battered (narrower at the top than the base) so the lower growth still gets enough light to stay dense.
  • A single clean annual cut being enough to hold a crisp, formal line, rather than repeated cuts through the season.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the North Downs chalk

The Burncoose video is a general nursery demonstration, not chalk-specific, so here is what that means locally.

Why yew belongs here. Yew is genuinely native to chalk downland across the North Downs, and Bridge sits right on that geology. It is moderately drought-tolerant once established, handles the thin, sharply alkaline topsoil on the downs above the village without complaint, and unlike some formal-hedge species it is not troubled by the valley-bottom frost pockets either. It is close to a no-compromise choice for this specific ground.

Timing and the nesting law. The September/October window Burncoose recommend sits well clear of the main bird nesting season under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (1 March to 31 August), which is one more reason it is the right annual slot, not just a horticultural preference.

Toxicity note for field boundaries. Yew clippings are toxic to livestock and horses. Around the farms and smallholdings towards Lower Hardres and Bishopsbourne, any yew arisings get bagged and removed, never left where grazing animals could reach them, which is standard practice but worth stating plainly.

Conservation-area check. Mature yew near Bifrons Park can easily exceed the 75mm section-211 threshold; I check stem diameter and file the Canterbury City Council notice where the hedge falls inside the CA boundary.
