Levelling an uneven hedge: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

An uneven hedge is one of the most common problems I am called out to fix around Bridge and the Nailbourne villages. The UK Gardening Tips channel has a straightforward guide to getting a wayward profile back under control, and this companion adds what a general video cannot: when the cut can legally happen around nesting season, which species in this part of Kent tolerate a corrective cut without stalling, and how the chalk slopes above Bridge behave differently from the wetter valley floor.

Watch first: How to Level an Uneven Hedge

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=ePVeeQuO4To

What the video shows

The video addresses the practical problem of a hedge that has developed a wavy, lopsided or generally inconsistent profile over several seasons of either no cutting or inconsistent cutting. The core techniques it demonstrates are:

  • Setting a reference string line. A stake at each end of the run, a taut string between them at the target height, and you now have a fixed datum to work to. The video is clear that measuring hedge height from ground level directly is unreliable if the ground is not perfectly flat, and in practice it rarely is. The string line removes that variable.
  • Applying a batter to the face. The hedge face should not be vertical: it should slope very slightly inward from base to top, so the crown is a little narrower than the base. The video explains why: a vertical or top-heavy profile shades the lower sections and starves them of light, which is why so many hedges end up bare at the base after a few years of flat-sided cutting.
  • Taking the corrective cut in stages. Where a hedge is badly out of profile, the video recommends removing material from the proud side only until it matches the setback target, then dressing the whole face consistently. Trying to level both sides at once in a single pass often leads to an under-cut hedge on one side while the other still needs work.
  • A second pass to catch missed areas. After the main cut, stepping back and looking along the line from one end shows any remaining high spots that a straight-on approach misses.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The nesting-season constraint, and why corrective work should not wait until spring. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. The practical window runs from 1 March through 31 August, with some late broods stretching into September. Corrective levelling that involves cutting into established structure, rather than just trimming new growth, can expose nest cavities that are invisible from the outside. For that reason I will not do structural correction work between March and August without a careful on-site survey first. October is a good month to deal with an uneven hedge: the nesting season is over, growth has slowed, and the hedge is still in good condition before the worst of the winter. The nesting-law timing guide covers the legal position in full.

Which species tolerate a corrective cut around Bridge. This matters because the right answer is not the same for every hedge. Beech (Fagus sylvatica), hornbeam (Carpinus betulus), yew (Taxus baccata), box (Buxus sempervirens), holly (Ilex aquifolium) and cherry laurel all regrow reliably from established wood and will accept the kind of corrective cut the video shows. Hawthorn and blackthorn, common on field boundaries in the Nailbourne valley, are similarly forgiving. Leylandii and most other conifers are a different matter: they do not regenerate from brown wood, so a corrective cut that goes past the green layer on a conifer hedge is permanent and can leave a dead face. I always check species before booking any levelling work, and for Leylandii I would not offer a structural correction on a hedge that has been left to develop significant brown patches on the face.

Chalk slope versus valley floor: growth rates and why it matters for profile. The chalk slopes running up towards Lower Hardres and Patrixbourne on the north side of the Nailbourne valley tend to be drier and produce somewhat slower, denser growth. A hedge on chalk may sit at roughly the same profile for three or four seasons before needing correction. The Nailbourne valley floor itself, and the gardens on the wetter ground around Bishopsbourne and Barham, produce noticeably faster growth in a wet spring. A hedge that was cut level in October can be 30 to 40 centimetres proud on one side by the following June if that side happens to be in slightly richer or wetter ground, or if it catches more sun. This is the main reason hedges around the valley floor tend to need more frequent checks against a string line than the same species would on chalk.

The batter is more important here than the video implies. The valley floor is a frost pocket: cold air drains down from the chalk downs on both sides and sits in the lowest ground. A hedge with a flat or slightly outward-sloping top holds snow, and a heavy snowfall on a flat-topped hedge can split established framework branches in a way that takes several seasons to recover from. I always put a slight inward taper on the top third of any hedge I am correcting on the valley floor, a little more pronounced than I would on a more exposed chalk site where wind matters more than snow loading. It is a small thing but it is the difference between a hedge that sheds a heavy fall cleanly and one that gets pulled apart by it.

Conservation area check before any significant reduction. If a hedge within or adjacent to the Bifrons Park Conservation Area has been allowed to grow to the point where individual stems have reached or exceeded 75mm diameter at the base, corrective work that involves cutting those stems down requires a six-week notification to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. This is worth checking before booking, particularly on older established boundaries near Patrixbourne Road where a hedge that has been left alone for several years can easily have developed stems of that size. A hedge reduction that starts as a levelling job can become a conservation-area notice job if nobody checks first.

Keeping tools to the task. A corrective levelling cut on a long run is quite demanding on blade edges. On established beech or hornbeam framework wood, you are cutting through denser material than new season growth, and a dull blade tears rather than cuts cleanly, which leaves a rougher face that is slower to heal. I sharpen before any levelling job and check the blade midway through a long run. The shears sharpening guide covers what that involves in practice.
