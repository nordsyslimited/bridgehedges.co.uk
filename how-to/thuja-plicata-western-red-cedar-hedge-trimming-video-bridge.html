Western red cedar (Thuja plicata) hedge trimming: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Thuja plicata, western red cedar, is the conifer hedge that gets planted when someone has had enough of Leylandii. Its growth rate is more measured, its foliage has a genuine cedar scent rather than a resinous one, and, crucially, it can regenerate from older wood in a way that Leylandii cannot. best4hedging, one of the UK's largest specialist hedging plant suppliers, have put together a clear species overview for Thuja plicata. This companion article takes their guide and adds what it cannot: the nesting-season timing for this ground, the skin-safety consideration that is specific to this species, how the chalk slopes and the Nailbourne valley floor behave differently, and what the High Hedges legislation means for a mature specimen around Bridge.

Watch first: A focus on Western Red Cedar hedging (best4hedging)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=cl7Pb9BAdzg  ·  Channel: best4hedging

What the video shows

best4hedging is a large UK online hedging plant retailer with a broad knowledge base built from their own stock, customer questions and years of species trials. Their Thuja plicata guide is part of the same "A focus on..." series as their Leylandii overview: honest, practical, and geared toward the garden boundary owner rather than a commercial grower. The video covers:

  • What western red cedar actually is. Thuja plicata is a North American native conifer in the cypress family, not a true cedar, introduced to British gardens as a hedging species in the nineteenth century. Its foliage is a rich, dark, glossy green with flat sprays that release a characteristic fresh, aromatic cedar scent when cut or brushed. The video makes the species easy to identify and distinguish from the similar-looking but considerably faster-growing Leylandii that often neighbours it on Kent boundaries.
  • Growth rate and what it means for maintenance. Western red cedar grows roughly 30-45cm per year in typical conditions, well below Leylandii's 60-90cm. That is not a slow hedge, but it is a manageable one, and it means that an annual or twice-yearly trim will reliably keep the hedge within a sensible envelope without requiring the precision timing that Leylandii demands.
  • The critical difference from Leylandii: it can regenerate from older wood. Toward the end of the video, the channel is clear that Thuja plicata, unlike Leylandii and unlike most other conifers, will generally regrow from bare, older wood when cut hard. This is the most practically important difference between the two species. It means that a Thuja hedge that has been allowed to run wide or tall can often be pulled back to a sustainable size, whereas the same operation on a Leylandii hedge leaves permanent dead patches. This is not unlimited: very old, very thick stems at the base of the hedge may not regenerate reliably, and the regrowth is slower than the first-year growth of a younger hedge. But the resilience is genuine and worth knowing before you book a job.
  • Trimming frequency and timing. The video advises one or two cuts per year for an established hedge. The main cut typically falls in late summer. The channel notes that cutting should stay within the green growth, particularly at the top, since any new growth needs time to harden before winter.
  • Common varieties. best4hedging covers the main cultivars in use: Atrovirens (darkest, most richly coloured, the most widely planted form for formal garden hedging), Brabant (lighter green, slightly faster), and Excelsior. For Bridge garden boundaries and estate approaches, Atrovirens is what I encounter most often.

The video does not address the nesting law, the skin-sensitivity issue specific to Thuja, the difference between chalk-slope and valley-floor growth rates, or the High Hedges legislation. Those are covered below.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Wear gloves, long sleeves and eye protection: Thuja plicata is a contact-sensitiser. This is worth saying plainly before anything else. Thuja plicata contains thujone and plicatic acid, compounds that cause contact dermatitis in some people and, with repeated exposure, can produce an occupational asthma response. This is not a theoretical risk: it is well documented in professional arboricultural and forestry workers. I wear nitrile gloves, long sleeves and eye protection when trimming any Thuja hedge, and I would recommend the same to anyone doing their own trimming. Fresh-cut Thuja arisings generate a fine spray of aromatic particles when processed through a chipper or compactor; handling them in quantity without protection is the scenario to avoid. The same caution applies, to a lesser degree, to cut foliage left on the ground. If you experience skin redness, itching or eye irritation during or after a Thuja trimming job, treat it as a sensitisation event and use full PPE for all future contact with the species.

The nesting-season window. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. The practical nesting period for most species in this area runs from 1 March through to the end of August, with some late broods extending into September. Thuja provides year-round cover and is used as a nesting site by a range of garden birds, particularly in the dense upper sections of a mature hedge. My practice for any Thuja boundary around Bridge is to do the main annual trim in September, after the nesting season has reliably closed and while there is still enough warmth for any new growth to firm up before winter. If a second lighter trim is needed in spring, I do a visual check for active nests immediately before starting. For the full legal position on nest disturbance, see the guide to hedge cutting timing and the nesting law.

Chalk slopes versus the Nailbourne valley floor. Bridge sits at the northern edge of the North Downs chalk. Thuja plicata is a good match for free-draining chalky soils: it tolerates dry conditions better than many conifers and does not suffer the yellowing on chalk that affects some less well-adapted species. On the drier chalk slopes running up towards Lower Hardres and Patrixbourne, a single annual trim in September will usually be sufficient for a mature hedge at a maintained height. On the heavier, moister ground of the Nailbourne valley floor, particularly around Bishopsbourne and Bekesbourne, Thuja grows noticeably faster. A valley-floor hedge will often need two cuts a year to stay within its envelope: a light trim in late May or early June after a nest check, and the main cut in September. The difference in growth rate between a chalk-slope and valley-floor Thuja hedge at Bridge is not as pronounced as the equivalent difference with Leylandii, but it is real and worth factoring into a maintenance plan.

The old-wood regeneration advantage in practice, and its limits. The fact that Thuja can regenerate from older wood does not mean you should let it run. A Thuja hedge that is well maintained at a sensible size is significantly easier to manage, and produces a cleaner result, than one that is cut back hard every few years. The regeneration capability is most useful when taking over an existing hedge that has not been maintained well, reducing a hedge that has been allowed to gain a metre or two beyond the ideal height, or correcting a hedge that is starting to thin at the base. When I am asked to reduce a Thuja hedge that has gone to 4 metres and needs to come back to 2.5 metres, the job is achievable in a way that the same job on a Leylandii hedge is not, provided there is still living green wood throughout the zone to be cut. Very old, brown-stemmed material at the base of a hedge that has not seen light for years is a different matter: I would inspect and advise on the specific hedge before quoting for any hard reduction. For a comparison of Thuja with Leylandii on these specific points, see the guide to Leylandii trimming for Bridge.

High Hedges and the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003. Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 gives a neighbour the right to complain to Canterbury City Council about any evergreen or semi-evergreen hedge over 2 metres that is adversely affecting the reasonable enjoyment of their property. Western red cedar, as an evergreen, falls within the Act in exactly the same way as Leylandii. The difference is that a Thuja hedge is easier to bring back down to a compliant height if it has gone over, because of its old-wood regeneration capacity. If a Thuja hedge on a neighbouring property is causing a problem, the process is the same as for any other evergreen: the guide to the high hedge complaint process covers the steps in full.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area. Any Thuja stems within or on the boundary of the Bifrons Park Conservation Area that have been allowed to grow as a screen rather than a maintained hedge can reach the 75mm stem-diameter threshold that triggers a six-week notice requirement to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Routine annual trimming of a hedge kept within the green zone does not raise this issue, but any significant reduction of an overgrown hedge, removal of specimens, or work on a specimen-grown Thuja tree does. I check the address against the CA boundary before quoting for any structural work.
