Field maple hedge planting: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Field maple (Acer campestre) is the UK's only native maple, and one of the most reliably successful hedge plants on the chalk and mixed soils of the North Downs. You see it in almost every old field boundary between Bridge and Patrixbourne, its lobed leaves turning yellow-gold through October before the hedge goes into its winter silhouette. Heathwood Nurseries, a UK specialist in native hedging, have produced a clear planting guide that covers the practical steps for getting field maple into the ground. What it cannot cover is this specific ground: the sap-bleed cutting window that catches people out each spring, the chalk-versus-valley-floor split around the Nailbourne, and the conservation-area checks that apply to any mature maple near Bifrons Park.

Watch first: How to plant a Field Maple Tree or Hedge – Heathwood Nurseries

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=7dJ4TrPJB8E  ·  Channel: Heathwood Nurseries

What the video shows

Wayne at Heathwood Nurseries walks through the process for planting a field maple hedge from nursery stock. The guide focuses on practical steps that apply to both a single specimen tree and a formal hedge run, and it is worth watching in full before you start. The main points it demonstrates:

  • Choosing the right stock. The video covers the difference between pot-grown and bare-root plants, and when each is appropriate. Bare-root field maple is typically available from November through to early April during the dormant season; pot-grown or cell-grown stock can go in at a wider range of times, provided moisture is managed.
  • Soil preparation before planting. Field maple is tolerant of a range of soils but, like all hedging, it benefits from breaking up compaction and removing competing grass or perennial weeds from the planting strip before the roots go in. The video shows the technique for a clean planting trench rather than individual holes, which is the right approach for a continuous hedge run.
  • Planting depth and spacing. For a formal single-row hedge, plants typically go in at roughly 30 to 45 centimetres apart, with the root collar at ground level. Planting too deep is a common cause of slow establishment; the video is clear that the nursery soil mark on the stem is the guide for depth.
  • Firming in and initial aftercare. Heeling the plant in firmly after backfilling removes air pockets that can desiccate roots. The video emphasises watering in immediately after planting, then monitoring moisture through the first growing season, particularly during any dry spell in late spring or early summer.
  • The formative prune after planting. The video covers cutting the newly planted plant back by roughly a third on planting to encourage a bushy, branched habit from the base rather than a single leader. This step is frequently skipped by people who feel reluctant to cut back something they have just planted, and it makes a significant difference to how dense the hedge becomes in its first two or three years.

The video does not address the timing risks specific to field maple, the ground conditions around Bridge, or any conservation-area rules. Those are the parts worth reading below.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The sap-bleed cutting window. Field maple, like all maples, will bleed sap from fresh cuts if pruned when sap flow is active. The risk period runs from roughly late February through to the end of April as the trees come out of dormancy. Hard or structural cuts done in that window will bleed persistently, which is not necessarily fatal to the plant but creates unnecessary stress and produces an unsightly result. I work to two safe windows for any structural work on field maple: the dormant season from mid-November through to late January, or the summer window in June and July once the growth flush has hardened off and sap pressure has dropped. Routine maintenance trimming of an established, actively growing hedge in August does not carry the same risk, because the cuts are minor and the plant is in full active growth, but I would not carry out a hard reduction in March. If someone books a job for early spring without mentioning the species, this is one of the first checks I make.

Field maple on chalk versus the Nailbourne valley floor. Field maple is one of the most versatile native hedging species for this part of Kent precisely because it works on both sides of the chalk-valley split. On the free-draining, alkaline North Downs above Bridge, where beech and yew are also reliable, field maple establishes confidently and grows steadily. It does not need the consistent moisture that some valley-floor species prefer, and it handles summer drought on chalk slopes better than many people expect. On the heavier, seasonally wetter valley floor between Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne and Bishopsbourne, field maple also performs well, tolerating clay and the occasional winter waterlogging near the Nailbourne rather better than beech. This versatility makes it a useful species to consider for a boundary that runs across both ground types, where a single-species hedge needs to cope with the transition. Compare this with beech, which I would not recommend for the wetter valley-floor sections, or with the bare-root planting guide for more on species choice by ground type.

Field maple as a wildlife hedge. Acer campestre is a native species with a long ecological history in the North Downs hedgerow network. The undersides of field maple leaves support colonies of aphids in summer, which in turn support ladybirds, lacewing larvae and the blue tit and long-tailed tit populations that work the hedgerows along the valley lanes. The winged seeds (samaras) ripen in October and are eaten by finches through the winter. A pure field maple hedge is less structurally complex than a mixed native boundary, but it holds wildlife value well, especially in the insect food-chain sense. If the goal is a formal, single-species hedge on a garden boundary, field maple is a more honest native alternative to the commonly planted but non-native hornbeam, and it colours up more dramatically in autumn. For a mixed native hedge alongside it, the mixed wildlife hedge planting guide covers the species combinations that work on both the chalk and the valley floor around Bridge.

Nesting season and cutting timing. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird, or to disturb an adult bird at or near an active nest containing young. The active nesting season runs from 1 March through to the end of August, with some late broods into September. Field maple is used for nesting by several species: blackbird and song thrush in particular will use a dense field maple hedge in the Nailbourne valley. The dormant-season planting window (November to January) sits well clear of this, as does the main formative pruning period. The summer maintenance trim window (June to July for sap reasons) coincides with the mid-nesting season, so I always check for active nests before any work on an established field maple hedge in summer, and I would halt the job if a nest with eggs or young were found. Early September is when I typically carry out routine summer trims on field maple, having checked for late broods.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area and mature field maple. A field maple that has grown from hedge planting over many years can reach a trunk or stem diameter above 75mm at 1.5 metres from the ground. At that point, any works involving cutting, uprooting or wilful damage require six weeks prior notification to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990, because the tree falls within a conservation area. The Bifrons Park Conservation Area covers land around Patrixbourne and parts of the Bridge parish boundary. Newly planted hedging is not subject to this, but a mature maple in an old boundary hedge certainly may be. If I am asked to reduce or reshape an old field maple boundary near Patrixbourne Road or the estate edges at Bifrons, I check the stem size and the CA boundary before confirming any booking.
