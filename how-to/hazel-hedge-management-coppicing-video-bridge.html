Hazel in your garden hedge: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Hazel (Corylus avellana) turns up in most mixed native hedges around Bridge and the Nailbourne valley, and in quite a few garden boundaries too. This guide takes an RHS film about the value of native hazel as its starting point, then adds what it doesn't cover: how to manage hazel when it is inside a clipped hedgerow rather than a woodland stool, the nesting-law timing, and what the chalk-slope and valley-floor conditions around Bridge actually mean for when and how you cut.

Watch first: Why you need a hazel in your garden | The RHS

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=hXi4IEH4ywY

What the video shows

Jonathan Newell, RHS Public Engagement Officer, makes the case for planting native hazel in a garden and covers the full annual cycle of what hazel does and why it matters. The film is worth watching before you decide how to manage any hazel in a boundary or mixed hedge, because the management decision should follow from understanding what the plant is doing. Key points he covers:

  • Hazel as a native British species and why that status matters for wildlife. The catkins appear from around January into early March, providing pollen at a point in the year when almost nothing else does. That early-season value is hard to replace with a non-native substitute.
  • The range of wildlife that depends on hazel: dormice use it as a key food plant and nest site, bank voles and wood mice take the nuts in autumn, and woodpeckers work the stems for the invertebrates in them. Around forty species of moth use hazel as a larval food plant in the UK.
  • Hazelnuts in autumn, and why leaving some on the plant rather than clearing everything supports the wintering bird population through October and November.
  • Coppicing as the traditional management approach: cutting the main stems close to the base encourages vigorous new shoot growth. The video covers the principle and explains that hazel produces its best growth on a rotation rather than an annual clip.

What the film doesn't address is what to do when your hazel is inside a clipped hedge boundary rather than a woodland stool or garden specimen. The management is different, and around Bridge the nesting-law timing and the chalk-ground conditions add a further layer that needs thinking through before you pick up a saw.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Clipped hedge hazel versus coppiced stool: different jobs, different tools. When hazel is part of a maintained mixed hedge, the approach differs from the woodland coppice the RHS film describes. A hedge hazel gets trimmed annually or every other year as part of the routine cut, and the framework stays narrow and even. That is fine and does no harm, but it means the hazel never produces the straight 2-4 metre rods that coppice rotation generates, and it provides somewhat less dormice and bat habitat than an unclipped or lightly managed boundary stool. Where a client wants to encourage wildlife in a field boundary, I will sometimes leave a section of hazel to develop more freely and coppice it on a 5-7 year rotation, with the clipped hedge running either side. That takes surveying first, and only works where the boundary is wide enough to accommodate a more natural stool.

Nesting-season law and the timing that catches people out with hazel. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 prohibits intentionally or recklessly damaging or destroying an active nest of any wild bird. The practical window for active nests runs from 1 March to the end of August, with some late broods extending into September. Hazel is a species where this matters earlier in the season than most people expect: some birds begin using dense hazel as a nest site from early March, and the same applies to the bank voles and dormice the RHS film mentions. If you are cutting boundary hazel in late February, check the base of the stems carefully before starting. The nesting-law timing guide covers the legal position in full.

When I schedule hazel work around Bridge. For a maintained boundary hedge that includes hazel as part of a mixed species planting, the best cutting window is late August through to late October: after nesting, before the nuts are fully taken and birds return to forage the hedge base. For hazel stems being coppiced properly (cut back hard to encourage rod growth), I work between November and February. The plant is fully dormant, catkins are just forming but not yet open, and nesting is not a real risk. Winter coppicing also means the stool regrows in spring without competition from established canopy shade, giving a much better result than a spring or summer cut.

North Downs chalk versus Nailbourne valley floor. Hazel grows on both, but differently. On the chalk slopes towards Lower Hardres and Patrixbourne, where the soil drains fast and dries out in a dry spring, hazel tends to grow more slowly and the stools are more compact. On the heavier, wetter ground of the Nailbourne valley floor around Bridge village itself, hazel can push strong upright stems that run well ahead of a maintained hedge line if left for two or more years in a wet season. That faster growth means a slightly earlier cut for a managed boundary, and it also means the coppice rotation in valley-floor positions can sometimes be shortened from 7 years to 5: the stools recover and produce usable rods more quickly. On either ground type, hazel copes better than most formal hedge species with the occasional waterlogging that the Nailbourne winterbourne brings.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area: when hazel needs a notice. Any hazel stems within or bordering the Bifrons Park Conservation Area that have been left unmanaged and are now multi-stem trees rather than trimmed hedge shrubs can reach the 75mm stem-diameter threshold that triggers the six-week notification requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. I have seen hazel in older estate boundaries near Patrixbourne Road with stems well over 100mm. Measuring before booking is essential in that area, and if notification is needed, the six-week window runs from the date Canterbury City Council receives it. For a standard maintained hedge hazel under 75mm, no notice is required. The conservation-area notice guide covers the process in full.

Hazel in a mixed native hedge planting. If you are planting a new boundary in the Nailbourne valley area, hazel is one of the best species to include alongside hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and dog rose. It establishes readily on both chalk slopes and valley-floor ground, grows quickly enough to fill a framework within a few seasons, and the annual cut keeps it tidy without sacrificing the wildlife value the RHS film describes. Aim for hazel at roughly 15-20% of any mixed planting rather than letting it dominate: it will outgrow slower neighbours like hawthorn in a wet season if the proportions are too high.
