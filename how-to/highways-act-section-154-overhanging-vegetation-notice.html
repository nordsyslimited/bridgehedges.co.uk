Received a section 154 notice in Bridge? What to do about an overhanging hedge or tree

A notice from the council says a hedge or tree of yours is hanging over the road or footpath and must be cut back. This guide sets out what to do first, how long you have (14 to 28 days), what happens if you let it lapse, and what section 154 of the Highways Act 1980 says.

Short answer: what to do about a section 154 notice

  1. Find the date to comply by. The Act says 14 days from service, and a notice can allow 14 to 28.
  2. Confirm the hedge or tree is yours. If it is not, tell the council in writing straight away.
  3. Photograph it and get it cut back in time. Kent County Council asks for 2.4m clear above a footway and 5.2m above the carriageway.
  4. Check for nesting birds, and for a protected tree, before any cutting.
  5. If you do nothing, the council can do the work and bill you. If you disagree, you can appeal to a magistrates' court, so act quickly.

General guidance, not legal advice

I cut hedges and prune trees for a living. This is the law as written plus what I see on the ground. If you dispute the notice or the cost looks large, speak to a solicitor or an advice service, and follow the wording of the notice you were sent.

What should I do if a section 154 notice lands?

  1. Read it carefully. Record who sent it, the date it was served, the date to comply by and the exact location. Photograph the notice.
  2. Confirm who owns the growth. The notice should go to the owner of the hedge, tree or shrub, or the occupier of the land it grows on. If it is a neighbour's, or stands on a verge or other land that is not yours, write to the sender immediately with whatever evidence you hold, such as your title plan. My neighbour's high hedge guide touches on the boundary side.
  3. Document it. Take dated photos from the road or footpath before cutting, with something in the frame to show height.
  4. Book the work now. The Act gives 14 days and some notices allow up to 28. Do not hold out for the last few days, as a wet week or a full diary can use up the margin.
  5. Look for nesting birds. A nest in use is protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. The Act sets no dates, but 1 March to 31 August is the accepted guidance window. If you find an active nest in the section to be cut, leave it, photograph it and tell the sender in writing so a new date can be agreed. My nesting law guide has more.
  6. Check tree protection. Garden hedges are normally fine. If the growth is a tree, or includes one, in a conservation area or under a tree preservation order, you must give notice or get permission first, and you should tell the sender. The conservation area tree notice guide walks through it.
  7. Cut back properly and clear up. Go back far enough that the next season's growth does not put you straight over the limits again, remove all cuttings from the road and path, and take dated photos afterwards.
  8. If you disagree, act at once. Contact the sender and explain. You can appeal to a magistrates' court, but the section gives no appeal deadline and does not say the compliance period stops during an appeal. Take advice before the date passes.

What does section 154 of the Highways Act 1980 provide?

  • Overhanging growth (subsection 1). Where a hedge, tree or shrub overhangs a highway, or another road or footpath the public can use, so as to endanger or obstruct vehicles or pedestrians, a notice can be served. It also applies where the growth obstructs a driver's view or the light from a public lamp, or endangers or obstructs horse riders on a highway.
  • Recipient. The owner of the hedge, tree or shrub, or the occupier of the land it grows on.
  • Time limit. The notice requires lopping or cutting within 14 days of service. Notices often allow more, up to 28 days, so go by yours.
  • Dead or dangerous growth (subsection 2). A similar notice can be served where a hedge, tree or shrub is dead, diseased, damaged or insecurely rooted and likely to fall onto the road or path. It requires cutting or felling within 14 days.
  • Appeal (subsection 3). A person aggrieved by the requirement may appeal to a magistrates' court.
  • Non-compliance (subsection 4). The authority may carry out the work and recover its reasonable expenses from the person in default.

The section is published in full on legislation.gov.uk.

Is it Kent County Council or Canterbury City Council?

The competent authority is the highway authority and, in non-metropolitan districts, the district council. Kent County Council is the highway authority for most roads in Kent, and Canterbury City Council is the district council covering Bridge. Kent County Council sets out its expectations on its overhanging vegetation page. Some councils write an informal request before issuing a formal notice. Treat that letter as a prompt to sort things out early.

How much clearance does the growth need?

Kent County Council's guidance asks for growth to be kept back so there is at least:

  • 2.4 metres (about 8ft) clear above a footway or cycle route.
  • 5.2 metres (about 17ft) clear above the carriageway.

It also asks for growth to be kept clear of the road edge and streetlights. These are the council's figures and not numbers from the Act, so if your notice sets different measurements, follow the notice.

Should I cut it myself or ask a professional?

Work beside a live road demands traffic awareness, proper equipment and care over where the carriageway begins. Kent County Council also cautions that damage to highway vegetation while cutting can leave you liable. For tall, thorny or roadside growth, a professional is the sensible choice. How can I avoid a repeat notice?

A hedge cut once or twice a year and a tree with its low branches taken back every few years seldom draws a letter. Do the road side after nesting has finished and keep it to the same line. Section 154 notice: common questions

A section 154 notice has arrived. What is the first thing to do?

Read the compliance date, then decide whether the hedge or tree is yours. Photograph it from the road or footpath, check for nesting birds and for any tree protection, and have it cut back before the date, keeping dated photos. If it is not yours, write to the sender at once. If you disagree with the notice, contact the council quickly and take advice, since you can appeal to a magistrates' court.

What if I simply do not act on the notice?

The authority can then do the work itself and recover its reasonable expenses from the person in default. The section does not itself set a fine, but the council's bill for the work will usually be well above the cost of arranging the cut yourself.

How many days does a section 154 notice allow?

The Act requires the cutting to be done within 14 days of the notice being served. Notices in practice can give anywhere from 14 to 28 days. Work to the date written on your notice.

Who has to deal with a hedge or tree that overhangs the footpath?

The notice is addressed to the owner of the hedge, tree or shrub, or the occupier of the land it grows on. If it is yours, it falls to you. If it belongs to a neighbour or stands on land you do not own, tell the sender in writing and include evidence.

Who can serve a section 154 notice around Bridge?

The Act names the highway authority and, in non-metropolitan districts, the district council. Kent County Council is the highway authority for most roads in Kent, and Canterbury City Council is the district council for Bridge. For a road or footpath that is not a highway, the local authority for the area can act.

Is there a right of appeal?

Yes. A person aggrieved by the requirement can appeal to a magistrates' court. The section sets no appeal deadline and does not say the compliance period stops while you appeal, so do not rely on either. Check the notice, ask the council and get advice early.

What clearance does the council expect above a path or road?

Kent County Council's published guidance asks for at least 2.4 metres clear above a footway or cycle route and 5.2 metres clear above the carriageway, with growth kept back from the road edge and streetlights. If your notice sets its own measurements, follow those.

Can I do the cutting myself?

If the hedge or tree is yours, yes, once you have checked for nesting birds and protected trees and can work safely beside traffic. Tall, thorny or roadside growth is usually best handed to a professional. Must I tell the council once the job is finished?

Kent County Council says that if you have a letter asking you to trim vegetation you do not have to tell them when it is done, as they check later. If you hold a formal notice with a deadline, keep dated photos of the completed work in case you are asked.
