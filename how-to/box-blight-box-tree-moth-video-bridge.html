Box blight and box tree moth: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

If the box hedging in your garden is going brown, bare or webbed over, the first job is telling the two main culprits apart: fungal blight and box tree moth caterpillar. David Domoney's video is the clearest single guide to that distinction. This companion adds what the video cannot: how the Nailbourne valley's damp microclimate affects blight risk, what removing failing box near Bifrons Park may require, and which alternatives work on Bridge's chalk.

Watch first: What is wrong with my Box Hedge? David Domoney explains.

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=49wGHoqzksQ

What the video shows

David Domoney is a Chartered Horticulturalist and one of the UK's most recognisable garden media figures: a presenter on ITV's Love Your Garden and a long-standing resident gardening expert on This Morning. His video addresses the two problems that have made growing box hedging genuinely difficult across England since the early 2010s. It covers:

  • How to tell box blight from box tree moth damage before doing anything else. Blight leaves dead leaves clinging to stems, often with black streaking on the stems themselves and white fungal spores visible in humid weather. Moth damage leaves behind webbing strung between leaves, and skeleton leaf fragments where caterpillars have fed through the canopy.
  • Box blight is caused by two closely related fungi, Calonectria pseudonaviculata and Calonectria henricotiae, which spread via water splash and thrive in warm, humid, poorly ventilated growing conditions.
  • Box tree moth caterpillar (Cydalima perspectalis) arrived in the UK around 2011 and has spread rapidly across England. The video shows how to find caterpillars inside the canopy and how quickly a generation can strip a hedge bare.
  • Management options for each problem: cutting back affected growth, improving airflow by thinning the canopy, treatment timing, and an honest assessment of when box is better replaced than nursed along.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

The video is a national guide aimed at the widest possible UK audience. Here is the layer that matters for this ground specifically.

Where blight risk runs higher around here. Box blight spreads by water splash, and the conditions it favours (warmth, humidity, limited airflow) suit the Nailbourne valley floor more closely than the open chalk slopes above Bridge. The valley can sit in low mist or damp overnight air while the ridge above is clear and dry, and enclosed settings such as walled or courtyard gardens near Bifrons Park concentrate that humidity. I tend to see more blight trouble in valley-bottom and sheltered walled settings than in open, well-aired gardens, and I flag that when I quote for work on box in those spots.

The moth has reached Kent. Box tree moth has been confirmed across Kent, including in Canterbury district, so treating it as a distant risk is not realistic any more. A methodical check at the start of each growing season, parting the canopy in late spring to look for webbing and caterpillars before the first clip of the year, is the best early warning system. Catching a first infestation early, before a full generation has stripped a section of hedge, makes the difference between a manageable setback and having to take it back hard.

Cutting timing and the nesting law. Box is dense, which is why it makes such a good formal hedge, and that same density makes it attractive to nesting birds. The same legal window applies as for any other hedging: section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest, with the safe cutting season running outside 1 March to 31 August. If you are cutting back blight-affected growth as a remediation measure rather than routine maintenance, check for active nests first regardless of time of year. The full nesting-law guide covers the detail, including what counts as a defensible check before you start.

Box near Bifrons Park Conservation Area. Mature box hedging within or bordering the Bifrons Park Conservation Area may form part of the recorded historic garden character. If you are considering removing or substantially altering box hedging in that area, it is worth checking with Canterbury City Council before proceeding. Section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 requires six weeks' notice to the local authority before carrying out work on trees or woody shrubs in a conservation area once stems reach 75mm at 1.5m height, and established box in old formal gardens can reach that threshold. Removal without notice where it applies is an offence, not just an oversight.

What to replace box with on chalk. If the box is too far gone and the decision is to start again, the most reliable alternatives on the free-draining chalk around Bridge are:

  • Ilex crenata (Japanese holly): fine-textured, clips to a formal line, immune to box blight and box tree moth, tolerates chalk reasonably well if given time to establish.
  • Lonicera nitida: fast to bulk up, slightly less formal in texture but tolerant of chalk and shade, useful for filling gaps quickly.
  • Sarcococca confusa (sweet box): winter-scented, very reliable on chalk, handles deep shade under other planting, not clipped to a tight formal edge but holds a reasonable shaped form.

I'd survey before recommending. Aspect and chalk depth vary considerably between the open village plots along the old Watling Street and the damper, shadier walled corners closer to the Nailbourne, and what suits one setting may not suit another.
