Olearia daisy bush pruning: a Burncoose video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Olearia is an underused but genuinely capable informal hedge or flowering screen for the right Bridge garden. On a free-draining chalk slope above the village, in a sheltered south- or west-facing position, the hardier species and cultivars produce a generous show of white daisy flowers in late May and June and need very little beyond a light cut once flowering is done. The limitation for inland Kent is frost hardiness: the tender Olearia scilloniensis that Burncoose Nurseries demonstrate in their summer-pruning video is not the best choice for the valley floor, but it is an excellent starting point for understanding how the genus works, and the same pruning approach applies to the hardier species you would actually plant in an exposed Bridge garden.

Burncoose Nurseries is a long-established specialist nursery and mail-order business based at the Caerhays Estate in Gwennap, Cornwall. They have an extensive YouTube channel of short, practical pruning demonstrations: their videos on yew, laurel, escallonia, lavender, mahonia and rosemary are already referenced elsewhere on this site. The olearia video is presented by Andrew from Burncoose, who demonstrates how to summer prune an Olearia scilloniensis immediately after flowering to encourage a denser, bushier habit and a better flower display the following year.

What the video shows

Andrew works through an Olearia scilloniensis shortly after it has finished flowering and demonstrates how a light summer cut keeps it in good shape. The main points the video covers:

  • When to prune: immediately after flowering. The video makes clear that the right moment is as soon as the flowers have dropped, while the plant still has the growing season ahead of it. Olearia flowers on the previous year's growth, so the aim of the post-flowering cut is to encourage the new season's side shoots, which will carry next year's flowers. Waiting until autumn or cutting in spring removes the embryo flower buds.
  • A light cut, not a hard reduction. Andrew takes back the flowered shoots to tidy the outline and promote bushy regrowth, without cutting deep into the older, woodier framework. Olearia scilloniensis does not regenerate reliably from very old stems, so the annual maintenance approach is to work within the younger growth rather than ever letting it get to the point of needing a hard renovation.
  • Encouraging density at the base. Early in the video, the reason for cutting back regularly each year is explained: left unpruned, olearia tends to become increasingly top-heavy and open at the base, with flowers that move progressively further from the centre of the plant. The annual post-flowering tidy is what keeps that base dense and productive.
  • How much to remove. The demonstration shows removing a moderate amount of the season's extension growth, keeping the overall shape rather than taking the plant back significantly. The goal is to encourage branching from lower on each stem, not to reshape the whole plant.

The video does not address frost hardiness differences between olearia species, the nesting-season law that sits across the pruning window, or which species works on an inland chalk-valley site rather than in Cornwall's mild Atlantic climate.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Species hardiness: O. scilloniensis versus O. x haastii for Bridge. Olearia scilloniensis is named after the Isles of Scilly, where the climate is mild and frost-free in most winters, and it reflects that origin in its hardiness. In an inland Kent garden, particularly on the lower Nailbourne valley floor where cold air collects and morning frosts persist well into spring, O. scilloniensis carries a real risk of hard winter damage in a severe year. It is not an impossible choice for a walled south-facing position in Bridge or Patrixbourne, but I would not plant it in an open position or on a frost-pocket site without a clear conversation about the risk first.

For most gardens in this area, Olearia x haastii (the compact hybrid daisy bush, RHS Award of Garden Merit) is a more practical starting point. It has smaller individual flowers than O. scilloniensis but produces them in similar abundance in July, tolerates considerably more frost, and makes an effective informal hedge on chalk-slope ground. The pruning approach is the same as the Burncoose video demonstrates, applied just after flowering in late July. Olearia macrodonta 'Major', with larger, serrated leaves and a bolder presence as a screen, is also worth considering for a sheltered chalk-slope position where something taller than 1.5m is needed; it is moderately hardy in SE England. Both are good value for a chalk-drained plot above Bridge village toward Lower Hardres or Bekesbourne, where drainage is reliable and south-facing gardens warm quickly. I would not use the more tender species on a cold, moisture-retaining valley-floor plot toward Bishopsbourne or Barham without adequate shelter and very free drainage.

Chalk versus the Nailbourne valley floor. Olearia is naturally adapted to well-drained conditions, and the free-draining chalk of the North Downs around Bridge suits it well: roots do not sit wet over winter, the soil warms relatively early in spring, and south-facing chalk garden walls can offer genuine microclimate protection for a slightly tender species. On the valley floor, the soil is heavier and retains more winter moisture, the frost pocket effect extends the period when the plant is under stress, and the combination of cold and wet is exactly what the tender species do not handle well. When I quote on a valley-floor garden near the Nailbourne where someone is thinking about olearia as a flowering screen, I am fairly direct about steering them toward O. x haastii over O. scilloniensis, or toward an alternative altogether if the site is notably cold or wet. The escallonia guide covers similar frost-hardiness choices for a comparable informal flowering hedge, with the same chalk-versus-valley-floor split.

The nesting-season window and summer pruning. Olearia scilloniensis flowers through May and into June. The correct post-flowering cut falls in June or early July, and O. x haastii's later July flowering pushes the ideal pruning window into late July and early August. All of these dates fall inside the nesting season under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which runs from 1 March to 31 August and makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The law does not prohibit summer cutting: it prohibits disturbing active nests. For a small ornamental shrub in a residential garden, the practical approach is to check carefully before cutting. Olearia foliage is reasonably dense and can harbour wrens and sparrows in an established plant, particularly at the lower levels where growth is thick. I check from the outside, then part the growth systematically before starting, looking for cup nests rather than assuming the plant is clear. By late July most first broods in the Bridge area have fledged, though second broods are possible through August on a productive site. The nesting-law timing guide explains what to look for and covers the full legal position.

Conservation area check for older specimens. Olearia grown as a garden hedge or border shrub and kept in regular maintenance is a shrub in the legal sense, not a tree, and routine pruning does not require notice from Canterbury City Council. If a specimen olearia on an older Bridge estate has been left unmanaged for many years and has developed thick, woody main stems, it is worth checking stem diameter before any structural work: any stem over 75mm measured at 1.5m above ground within or immediately bordering the Bifrons Park Conservation Area triggers the six-week notification requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. In practice this threshold is rarely met on a routinely managed olearia, but it is worth a brief check on any mature, neglected specimen before booking a hard renovation cut.

When a chalk-slope olearia becomes a hedge. Olearia x haastii planted at 60cm spacing and kept clipped annually makes a presentable low-to-medium informal hedge, and the July flower display is more consistent on chalk than most gardeners expect when they first see it quoted. It is not a formal clipped hedge in the yew or hornbeam sense: the flower buds form on the previous year's growth and a formal high-frequency trim would remove them. The right approach is what the Burncoose video demonstrates for O. scilloniensis: one disciplined cut immediately after flowering each year, light enough to preserve the framework while encouraging new side shoots. Kept on that rhythm, an O. x haastii hedge at around 1m tall is relatively low maintenance and free of the frost anxiety that accompanies the more tender species. For a lower-growing option on the same chalk-slope site, the small-leaved hebes make a useful companion planting or alternative front-border hedge; the hebe pruning guide covers the cultivar choices and what to expect from pruning timing on chalk. If you are considering olearia for a chalk-slope border or screen at a Bridge or Patrixbourne property and are not sure which species suits the position, I am happy to look at the aspect and soil before recommending.
