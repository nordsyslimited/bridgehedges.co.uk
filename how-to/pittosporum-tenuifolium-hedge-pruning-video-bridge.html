Pittosporum tenuifolium hedge pruning: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Pittosporum tenuifolium has become one of the more visible hedging plants in Bridge CT4 gardens over the past decade. It makes a dense, upright evergreen screen, tolerates moderate clipping, and its popular cultivars carry variegated or dark-toned foliage that reads differently in a garden border from the formality of hornbeam or yew. The variegated forms, particularly 'Silver Queen', 'Irene Paterson' and 'Garnettii', are the most commonly planted as hedges in this area. Burncoose Nurseries, the established Cornish specialist whose pruning guides are referenced elsewhere on this site, has produced a clear demonstration of the most critical maintenance task for any variegated hedging plant: identifying and removing green reversions. That is the video this guide is built around. The reversion technique it shows transfers directly to variegated pittosporum, and the local angles, specifically frost hardiness, the difference between the chalk slope above Bridge and the Nailbourne valley floor, the nesting-season timing conflict for the main summer cut, and what the Bifrons Park Conservation Area means for older specimens, are what this guide adds to the general picture.

Watch first: Pruning Variegated Plants (Burncoose Nurseries)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=4WSjAk_xzCA  ·  Channel: Burncoose Nurseries

What the video shows

Burncoose Nurseries is one of the UK's largest specialist plant nurseries, based at Gwennap in Cornwall, with an RHS Gold Medal record and a wide catalogue of less-common ornamental shrubs and trees. Their YouTube channel produces practical plant-care demonstrations for the species they sell and grow. This guide to pruning variegated plants focuses on the maintenance problem every owner of a variegated hedge or screen plant will eventually face: reversion, where individual shoots revert to the plain green form of the species and begin to outgrow the variegated growth around them.

  • What a reversion looks like. Early in the video, Burncoose shows what a reverted, plain-green shoot looks like against the background of a variegated plant. The plain green growth is typically more vigorous: the shoots are longer, the leaves larger or more intensely coloured than the variegated ones around them. This difference in vigour is precisely why reversions are a problem. Left in place, they tend to outgrow the variegated growth steadily and, over several seasons, can dominate the whole plant.
  • Why mid-stem cuts do not work. The video makes clear that cutting a reversion mid-stem does not eliminate it. Dormant buds lower on the same stem will break and push again from below the cut, often producing multiple new shoots. The only effective approach is to trace the reverted shoot back to its point of origin in the main framework and remove it completely at that junction.
  • How to find the junction. The demonstration shows the process of tracing a reverted shoot back through the canopy to where it emerges from the main stem. This takes care in a dense hedge. The junction is where the whole shoot needs to come out, and the cut is made cleanly there, leaving no stub that could re-push.
  • What the plant looks like after the work. Toward the end of the video, Burncoose shows the result: the variegated character of the plant is restored, and the remaining canopy is consistent in colour and texture. Regular checking and prompt removal of reversions at this stage, while they are still single stems without side branches, keeps the task straightforward. A plant that has been left for two or three seasons with unchecked reversions requires considerably more careful work to restore.

The video does not address the timing for a general maintenance trim, frost hardiness, the differences between a mild Cornish climate and an inland Kent chalk valley, or the specific requirements of pittosporum tenuifolium in a garden setting. Those are the parts worth reading on.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Reversion removal on variegated pittosporum: when and how often. The technique demonstrated by Burncoose applies directly to the variegated pittosporum tenuifolium cultivars planted as hedges and screens in this area. 'Silver Queen', 'Irene Paterson' and 'Garnettii' are all prone to producing plain green reversions, particularly after a hard winter, an unusually dry summer, or in sections of the hedge that receive less light. The best time to check and remove reversions is late spring, once the new season's growth has broken and the green shoots are clearly distinguishable from the variegated background. I try to do a reversion pass on any variegated pittosporum I maintain at that point in the season, before the shoots have grown long enough to have produced their own side branches, which makes tracing them back harder. The plain green form of pittosporum tenuifolium is sold in its own right, and it makes a reliably hardy, clean-cut hedge. But if the client specifically planted a variegated form for the foliage effect, keeping that character requires a check each growing season, not just occasionally.

Timing for the general maintenance cut and the nesting-season question. Pittosporum tenuifolium hedges in garden conditions typically need one or two maintenance trims per season, not the frequent clipping that box or yew require. The standard UK advice is to cut in late spring (May to early June) for the first trim, and again in late August if a second cut is needed to tighten the profile. Both windows carry a nesting-season overlay. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. The protected period runs from 1 March to 31 August. A late August cut falls just inside the end of that window, and pittosporum's dense, sheltered interior makes it a viable nesting site for wren, robin and blackbird late in the season. The approach I take is to push the second-cut timing into the first week of September if the hedge can wait. A week or two of extra growth is never significant on a pittosporum that is not already overgrown, and moving the cut past the 31 August line removes the legal risk. The nesting-law and cutting-timing guide covers the survey steps and the full legal position in detail.

Frost hardiness: chalk slopes versus the Nailbourne valley floor. Pittosporum tenuifolium is rated H4 on the RHS hardiness scale, tolerant of temperatures down to around minus 10 degrees Celsius. That places it in broadly the same category as escallonia and griselinia: reliable in a sheltered UK garden position in most winters, but vulnerable in an unusually hard season or in an exposed, frost-pocket position. On the free-draining chalk slopes above Bridge, with a reasonable south or west aspect and some protection from the prevailing wind, pittosporum tenuifolium performs well and comes through most winters without serious damage. The Nailbourne valley floor is a different matter. The valley is a cold-air drainage channel: frost collects in the lower sections, particularly the flat ground towards Bishopsbourne, Barham and Kingston, and can remain there for hours after the surrounding chalk slopes have warmed up. A pittosporum planted on the lower valley floor, in an exposed aspect with no overhead shelter, is at noticeably greater frost risk than the same plant twenty metres up the chalk slope. I have seen valley-floor pittosporum that looked dead after a cold February recover and push from the base in May, but the plant lost the full season's display, and older specimens with thick established stems take the frost harder than younger plants. When I am asked to plant a new pittosporum hedge in a low-valley position, I discuss the frost risk clearly and sometimes suggest griselinia as the safer evergreen alternative where frost tolerance is the main concern.

Variegated forms: marginally less hardy than the green species. The variegated cultivars of pittosporum tenuifolium are, as a general principle, slightly more frost-sensitive than the plain green species. The coloured forms tend to have somewhat thinner-textured foliage and slightly less vigorous root systems. This does not mean they will fail in a Kent garden: in a sheltered chalk-slope position in Bridge or Patrixbourne, the popular variegated cultivars perform reliably. But on the cold side of a valley-floor garden with no shelter belt, the difference matters. 'Tom Thumb', a compact, dark-purple-leaved selection of tenuifolium, is surprisingly hardy in comparison with many of the cream-variegated cultivars, and I would use it in a frost-exposed position where the client specifically wants the tenuifolium habit. The plain green species itself makes a clean, relatively fast-growing formal hedge and is the most reliably hardy option across the range.

Pittosporum as a box substitute on chalk. Some gardens in Bridge and Patrixbourne have lost box hedges to blight or box tree moth and want something with a similar compact, formal character. Pittosporum tenuifolium is used as a substitute in this context. It is a reasonable replacement in terms of density, response to clipping, and the ability to hold a formal line, though it grows faster than box and needs more frequent maintenance to stay tight. It does not have the fine-textured leaf that makes mature box distinctive at close range, and it is somewhat less tolerant of summer drought on chalk than box is. The box blight and box tree moth guide covers the box-replacement decision in more detail if that is the starting point for the conversation.

Bifrons Park Conservation Area and older pittosporum specimens. Pittosporum tenuifolium grows relatively quickly in a Kent garden in good conditions, and an established hedge planted in the 1990s or early 2000s can carry stems of significant diameter. Any woody shrub or hedge within the Bifrons Park Conservation Area boundary with stems above 75mm diameter at 1.5m height falls under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Canterbury City Council administers the notification for the Bridge parish. Six weeks' prior written notice is required before any work can begin. A regular maintenance trim on a young pittosporum hedge rarely produces stems anywhere near that threshold. A renovation or hard reduction on a large, old specimen is a different matter: I check the CA boundary and the stem diameter before booking any structural work. The conservation-area notice guide covers the notification process step by step.
