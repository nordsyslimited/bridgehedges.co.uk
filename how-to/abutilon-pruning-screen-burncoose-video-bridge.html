Abutilon as an informal flowering screen: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Abutilon (sometimes called flowering maple) is not a hedge in the clipped formal sense, but on a south-facing wall or in a sheltered corner in Bridge or the Nailbourne valley villages it makes one of the most striking informal flowering screens of the summer season. The question most people bring me is whether they can prune it hard and when. This Burncoose Nurseries video gives the core answer; here is the Bridge-specific layer on top of it.

Burncoose Nurseries in Cornwall, part of the Caerhays Estate, runs one of the UK's most respected specialist plant nurseries, particularly well known for tender and near-hardy shrubs that push the boundaries of UK outdoor growing. Their short pruning guides are produced at the nursery by their own team and built on direct growing experience with the plants they sell. For a subject like abutilon, where UK hardiness is the central question for most gardeners, that practical plant-nursery authority makes their advice worth taking seriously.

What the video shows

The Burncoose guide focuses on the single most important thing most gardeners get wrong with abutilon: how hard to prune it in spring. The key points it demonstrates are:

  • Spring is the right time, and hard pruning is correct. Burncoose are clear that abutilons benefit from quite severe pruning in spring, once the risk of frost has passed. This runs counter to the instinct many gardeners have to prune lightly for fear of harming the plant. Abutilon responds well to being cut back hard and pushes strong new flowering growth from the pruned wood.
  • The timing trigger is frost clearance, not a fixed calendar date. The guide ties the pruning window to the end of frost risk rather than to a month. In Cornwall's mild climate this may mean February or early March; in a colder UK garden the same logic applies but the trigger date moves later.
  • What to remove. Weak, spindly and frost-damaged growth is taken out first. Remaining stems are then cut back to encourage strong shoots from lower on the plant. This keeps the framework compact and prevents abutilon from becoming a leggy tangle of thin stems, which is what happens when the plant is left unpruned over several seasons.
  • Wall-trained abutilon. The video addresses how to keep a wall-trained specimen within its allotted space: cutting back the longest shoots to a lateral bud, working in from the edges to maintain the flat wall plane. This applies directly to the most common use of abutilon in a Kent garden, trained against a south or south-west facing wall.
  • The payoff. Pruning hard in spring produces the compact framework from which abutilon pushes its best flowering season. Skipping the spring cut, or cutting too lightly, means progressively fewer flowers carried on increasingly thin and gappy growth.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Burncoose is in Cornwall, one of the mildest parts of the UK, so the frost timing in their guide does not translate directly to a Nailbourne valley garden. Here is what changes in Bridge.

Hardiness and which form to plant. Abutilon vitifolium and its hybrid Abutilon x suntense (which includes well-known named forms such as 'Jermyns Blue' and 'Tennant's White', both raised in Hampshire) are the most reliably hardy forms for a Kent wall. The RHS rates them H4, meaning they can cope with temperatures down to roughly -5 to -10 degrees Celsius, which covers most Bridge winters on a sheltered south-facing wall but leaves them exposed in a severe winter or an unprotected position. The small-flowered lantern types, Abutilon megapotamicum and A. x milleri, with their pendulous red-and-yellow bells, are H3 or below: marginal in the open in this part of Kent, and really need a frost-free wall with deep shelter or indoor overwintering. For a new planting in Bridge I would steer toward A. vitifolium forms or A. x suntense rather than the tender lantern types unless the wall aspect is exceptionally sheltered and south-facing with an overhanging eave.

The frost-pocket timing problem. The Burncoose guide says to prune once frost risk has passed. In Cornwall that may mean late February. In Bridge the picture is more variable. The chalk slopes above the village, with good air drainage down toward the valley, often clear their frost risk by late March. The Nailbourne valley floor, particularly the lower reaches toward Bishopsbourne and Barham, can hold late-season ground frosts into April and sometimes the first week of May. I do not prune abutilon in a valley-floor garden until I am confident the frosts are done: a fresh hard prune followed by a sharp April frost can kill the new buds and set the plant back significantly. The safe rule for the valley floor is to wait until growth has visibly started before cutting. For the chalk slopes, late March is usually fine in a normal year, though I would still check the forecast for the week ahead before making the cut.

The nesting-law window. Abutilon grown against a wall or as a free-standing screen is exactly the kind of dense, sheltered structure that robins, wrens and house sparrows use for a nest. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active nest, and the main nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The spring pruning window for abutilon, which runs from late March through to April or May depending on frost clearance, falls within that protected period. I always survey the plant carefully before the first cut: working slowly across the wall growth, checking within the framework for any nest material or eggs. If a nest is in use, the work stops until the birds have finished and the young have left, which may push the pruning into May or June. This is not ideal for the plant but is far preferable to committing an offence or destroying an active nest. The nesting-law and cutting-timing guide covers the survey approach and legal position in full.

Wall aspect and what to look for in Bridge. The older properties along the lanes around Bridge, Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne and Bishopsbourne frequently have south or south-west facing walls: chalk-rubble or old brick construction that absorbs and re-radiates warmth through the growing season. These are the positions where abutilon vitifolium and A. x suntense are worth planting. A north or east-facing wall is not suitable. An unprotected free-standing position on the valley floor is marginal. The right combination of a warm wall and the relatively mild and well-drained character of the North Downs chalk belt gives the plant a genuinely good chance without needing the cossetted conditions it requires in colder or wetter parts of the UK. If you have a south-facing wall that already shelters a ceanothus, it will usually suit an abutilon too; the ceanothus pruning guide covers the timing rules for that common wall-companion plant.

Conservation-area check for mature specimens. Abutilon vitifolium can develop a substantial main stem over ten or fifteen years on a sheltered wall, particularly if it has been left without hard annual pruning. Any woody stem that reaches 75mm in diameter at 1.5 metres above ground, within or immediately adjacent to the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, requires a six-week prior-notification to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 before significant pruning work begins. This is not a concern for a recently established or regularly pruned plant, but for a mature neglected specimen that you are about to cut back hard after a gap of several years, it is worth a check. The conservation-area notice guide explains how to check the boundary and submit the notification if it is needed.
