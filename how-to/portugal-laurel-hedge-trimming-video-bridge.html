Portugal laurel hedge: trimming and growing Prunus lusitanica around Bridge, Kent.

Portugal laurel is the quieter cousin of cherry laurel: smaller leaves, a more refined appearance, and a better temperament on shallow chalk. Best4Hedging, a UK hedging nursery, produced a practical overview of what Prunus lusitanica offers as a hedging plant. This guide takes their demonstration and adds what it cannot cover from a distance: how Portugal laurel performs on the North Downs chalk above Bridge, on the damper Nailbourne valley floor, and inside the Bifrons Park Conservation Area.

Watch first: A focus on Portuguese Laurel, Best4Hedging

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=SGWwhP9L-Yc (Best4Hedging)

What the video shows

Best4Hedging is a UK hedging nursery selling and advising on planting stock for domestic and commercial boundaries. Their overview of Prunus lusitanica is practical nursery guidance, focused on why the species earns a place in a UK hedge plan and how to keep it in good order. The video covers:

  • Why Portugal laurel is chosen as an alternative to cherry laurel for formal boundary hedges: its smaller, darker leaves give a cleaner face after trimming, and it tends to hold its shape between cuts better than the faster-growing common laurel species.
  • Growth characteristics: Portugal laurel is a moderate grower, putting on roughly 20 to 40 cm a year once established. That is slower than cherry laurel, which is often presented in the video as the point rather than a drawback. A hedge that grows moderately is one that needs trimming twice a year rather than three or four times, and stays closer to its intended height without constant intervention.
  • Hardiness and soil tolerance: the species is reliably hardy across the UK, tolerating cold winters, and performs well on a wide range of soil types including the shallow chalky soils that prove difficult for many other evergreens. It also copes with shade, which is noted as a useful quality for a boundary hedge that may spend part of the day in shadow from buildings or larger trees.
  • The scented white flowers in May and June, which the video presents as a genuine advantage over species that flower less freely or not at all when regularly clipped. These appear on the current season's wood, toward the end of the branches, which has implications for timing the annual trim.
  • Trimming guidance: twice yearly is the standard recommendation, with the main cut after the spring flush in June or July and a tidying pass in early autumn if the hedge has put on uneven growth. The video notes that Portugal laurel's smaller leaf size means a hedgetrimmer gives a much cleaner result than is possible on cherry laurel, where the larger leaves suffer brown edges when cut across with a reciprocating blade.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Best4Hedging's overview is a nursery guide for a general UK audience. Here is what changes when the hedge is in Bridge, Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne, Barham, Kingston or the other Nailbourne valley villages.

Chalk soils and the North Downs slope. Portugal laurel is one of the better-suited evergreen hedging species for the shallow chalk on the slopes above Bridge village. Where cherry laurel can struggle at the thinnest points of the chalk layer, Prunus lusitanica copes more reliably and is less inclined to show yellowing foliage when the roots hit chalk at 20 to 30 cm depth. I have quoted for and planted Portugal laurel hedges on properties on the upper part of Bridge Hill specifically for this reason: it tolerates the free-draining, alkaline conditions that the chalk and flint layer imposes, and it does not need irrigation once it gets through the first summer. The contrast with the heavier, moisture-retentive alluvial ground in the valley bottom is marked: on valley-floor properties the growth rate can edge toward the upper end of the 20 to 40 cm range, particularly in wet springs when the Nailbourne rises. Either way the species remains manageable without a significant difference in approach.

The nesting season and the May to June flowering conflict. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. The nesting season runs practically from 1 March to 31 August, and Portugal laurel hedges attract nesting birds for the same reasons that make them popular as boundary hedges: dense, dark and impenetrable from the outside. The timing guidance in the video, which recommends the main cut in June or July, therefore puts the job squarely inside the legal window. I always check for active nests before trimming from March through to the end of August. Where a hedge is visibly used for nesting I defer the cut until September. The practical implication is that customers who want to preserve the flowering display should also expect to wait until after nesting season closes before I touch the hedge: attempting a post-flowering cut in late June or July while nests may still be active is not a route I recommend. The nesting-season guide covers the legal position in full.

Autumn cut timing and the Nailbourne winter. An early autumn tidying pass, say late September or early October, works well on Portugal laurel around Bridge once the nesting season is firmly closed. This gives the hedge time to present a tidy face through winter without a late cut that might coincide with the first sharp frosts. The Nailbourne valley sits in a frost pocket: cold air drains off the chalk slopes above Patrixbourne and collects on the valley floor, and frosts can arrive noticeably earlier and harder here than on the slopes. Portugal laurel is rated hardy to around -15°C, so a normal Kent winter does it no harm at all, but cutting in October also avoids the risk of a heavy trim just before the first frost of the season, which would leave fresh wounds open when temperatures drop. For a hedge that had a normal main cut in July, this is rarely a concern; for one that was deferred to September because of nesting birds, I keep the autumn pass light.

Difference from cherry laurel: tool choice and arisings. The video's point about tool choice is the most practically useful piece of guidance for customers deciding between Portugal laurel and cherry laurel. Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) has large, leathery leaves that a reciprocating hedge trimmer cuts through mid-blade, leaving brown desiccated edges that look bad for weeks. Secateurs or a single-blade tool are needed for a clean finish on cherry laurel. Portugal laurel's leaves are considerably smaller: a standard petrol or battery hedgetrimmer produces a genuinely clean cut, which means the job is faster and the result looks tidier for longer. The arisings are also easier to bag: smaller leaf fragments compact better in a garden waste bag than the large slabs of laurel leaf that cherry laurel produces. One point worth noting: Portugal laurel, like all Prunus species, contains cyanogenic glycosides in its leaves and bark, so I bag the clippings and remove them off site rather than leaving them in a heap on a rural property where livestock could access them.

Conservation-area check. Portugal laurel hedges in the Bifrons Park Conservation Area around Bridge centre can reach a stem diameter that triggers the notice requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Routine annual maintenance trimming of established topgrowth does not generally require a notice, but where I am reducing height substantially, taking off significant side material, or removing an established specimen, I check with Canterbury City Council before starting work. The six-week notice period under s.211 applies to any tree or large shrub with a stem above 75 mm at ground level within a designated conservation area. The conservation area notice guide covers the process in detail.
