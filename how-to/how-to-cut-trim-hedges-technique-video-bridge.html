How to cut a hedge: technique, batter and direction, a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Most hedge-cutting problems come down to technique rather than the tool or the species. Getting the working direction wrong leaves a hedge that leans outward at the top, blocks its own light at the base, and gradually opens up at the bottom over several seasons. Lee Burkhill (Garden Ninja) made a comprehensive UK hedge-cutting guide that covers the fundamentals cleanly. This companion adds what his video does not cover: how those fundamentals land on the chalk slopes of the North Downs, in the moister growth conditions of the Nailbourne valley floor, and against the nesting-season timing that shapes every cutting job in this area between March and August.

Lee Burkhill, known as Garden Ninja, is an award-winning UK garden designer and BBC Garden Rescue presenter. His channel covers practical cutting technique from a professional standpoint rather than a purely horticultural one, which makes his advice directly applicable to the kind of boundary hedges and formal screens that make up the bulk of the work in Bridge, Patrixbourne, Bishopsbourne and the Nailbourne villages. His hard pruning guide is used elsewhere on this site for photinia renovation; this is a different video covering general technique rather than a single species.

What the video shows

Lee works through hedge cutting from tool selection to the finished surface. The core points that matter regardless of species or location:

  • Always cut the sides bottom to top. Working upward lets cut material fall away from the blade continuously rather than piling on the cutting head. Working from the top down pushes clippings into the section below and can double-cut material, which slows the work and leaves a rougher surface. The upward sweep also naturally produces a cleaner angle on the finished face.
  • Cut the sides before the top. Finishing the two faces first makes it easier to judge where the top line is, because the finished height is set by what remains between the two cut faces. Cutting the top before the sides risks taking too much off one side when you come to level it up.
  • Batter the profile: narrower at the top, wider at the base. A hedge cut with vertical faces may look tidy immediately after cutting, but the upper growth shades out the lower growth. Within a few seasons the base thins, loses density and starts to show a gap at the bottom. A slight inward taper on both faces, with the base 10 to 15 centimetres wider than the top, keeps light reaching the lower growth year round. The video demonstrates the principle clearly and it is one of the most consequential habits in hedge maintenance.
  • Use a string line for a straight top. Two canes set at the intended finished height with a string pulled tight between them takes five minutes to set up and eliminates the gradual drift that happens when you are working along a long run and trying to judge level by eye. The string does not need to stay up for the entire job; it sets the reference point, particularly at the two ends and the middle.
  • Check for nesting birds before starting. The video includes a reminder to check the hedge thoroughly for active nests before cutting, which is a legal requirement under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 during the nesting season. The check should cover the full length of the hedge at close range, particularly in dense sections, before the first blade stroke.
  • Thorny species need heavy gloves throughout. Berberis, hawthorn and pyracantha all have spines that will go through light garden gloves. Lee makes the point that this is not optional and that injuries from these species happen most often when clearing fallen clippings by hand after cutting, not during the cut itself.
  • Tool choice by hedge type. Powered hedge trimmers are the right choice for straight formal hedges and large areas; hand shears or secateurs give cleaner results on loose informal screens, on cuts near property boundaries where precision matters, or on species with large leaves where a trimmer leaves visible cut edges. The video addresses both and is clear that neither tool is universally correct.

What the video does not address is how these principles interact with the specific growth conditions of the North Downs chalk, the differences between a chalk-slope garden and a Nailbourne valley-floor site, the nesting-season timing window in detail, or what changes when you are working on a slope rather than flat ground.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Chalk slope versus valley floor: how much growth you are actually managing. The growth rate of an established hedge depends heavily on the soil. On the chalk slopes above Bridge, toward the ridge at Lower Hardres and down the lanes to Patrixbourne, the topsoil is thin, sharply alkaline and free-draining. Hedges in this ground grow more slowly than the same species planted in the deeper, moister alluvial soil of the Nailbourne valley bottom around Bishopsbourne and Barham. In practice this means a privet or hawthorn hedge on the chalk will often produce 20 to 30 centimetres of new growth in a season; the same species on a well-watered valley-floor plot can produce twice that. The technique in the video is the same either way, but the volume of material being removed and the frequency with which the job is needed are different. When I quote valley-floor hedges I expect to cut two or three times through the season; chalk-slope work is more often a single annual cut with one light tidy.

Getting the batter right on a sloped site. The batter principle (wider base, narrower top) is straightforward on flat ground. On a sloped site, particularly on the downward-falling chalk faces toward Patrixbourne and Bekesbourne, the visual reference changes: a hedge that is correctly battered relative to vertical will look as though it leans uphill when viewed from below the slope. The fix is to set the batter relative to true vertical rather than relative to the slope, which means checking the finished profile from directly in front rather than from downhill. A string line and a level on the cane tell you where vertical is on a steeply sloped garden; the eye alone will usually lead you into cutting a wall that leans with the slope rather than standing plumb.

The nesting-season timing: when technique and law intersect. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. The main nesting period runs from 1 March through to 31 August, with late broods of some species extending into September. This period covers most of the standard cutting season for deciduous hedges and all of the summer trimming season for evergreens. The legal position does not prohibit cutting during this period: it prohibits disturbing active nests. My practice is to do a close physical check of the full hedge length before starting, specifically looking for occupied nests in the sections I am about to cut. On a dense mature hawthorn boundary that has not been cut for two or three seasons, this check takes longer than on a recently maintained privet screen. For the full legal position and what to look for during a check, the nesting-law and timing guide covers it in detail.

When to reach for hand shears rather than a trimmer. Garden Ninja's guide covers both tool types, but the choice is not always obvious. Around Bridge and the Nailbourne villages there are several species and situations where I consistently use hand tools rather than a powered trimmer. Yew, which appears frequently on the chalk slopes and on older estate boundaries, holds a crisper edge when cut with hand shears; the reciprocating blade of a powered trimmer can bruise the foliage tips on yew and leave a brown fringe that persists for a few weeks. Box topiary work in garden features near Bifrons Park is almost always hand-shear work, partly for precision and partly because the vibration of a powered trimmer on a small ball or cone form is difficult to control. Any hedge in a position where a powered blade cannot reach safely, particularly tall work above head height or work at height on a ladder, should not be attempted with a trimmer: hand shears or secateurs used at the right height on a stable platform are safer. The hedge shear sharpening guide covers what it takes to keep hand tools in cutting condition.

Working around large hedges in stages. The batter and direction technique works equally well whether you are maintaining a hedge that is already in good shape or working a hedge that has been left untouched for several seasons. The difference on a neglected hedge is that the batter may need to be rebuilt rather than simply maintained: a hedge that has been cut vertically for years, or not cut at all, will typically have heavier top growth shading out a thinning base. Rebuilding the batter in this situation usually means two or three seasons of progressively correcting the profile rather than trying to establish the correct angle in a single cut. Attempting a full profile correction in one pass risks cutting back further on the sides than the new growth can easily recover from, particularly on slower-growing species such as yew or beech. I explain this at the quote stage so the expectation is set correctly: the first cut after a period of neglect improves the hedge, but the full profile correction is a two-season job.

Conservation-area work near Bifrons Park. Routine maintenance cutting of hedges, including the annual or twice-yearly trim, does not require notice under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 regardless of location. The notice requirement applies to works to trees with a trunk of 75mm diameter or above at 1.5 metres, in or immediately adjacent to a conservation area. Established hedge shrubs maintained on a regular cutting cycle almost never reach that threshold; the occasional very old multi-stem hawthorn or yew at the base of a boundary hedge can, particularly near the older estate boundaries around Bifrons. If the material being cut includes anything that looks structural rather than shrubby, I measure at 1.5 metres before booking. The conservation-area notice guide covers the process for Canterbury City Council notifications.
