Tree work in a Bridge conservation area: a video companion, then the notice step by step.

Anyone asking how to get permission to prune a tree in Bridge is really asking two questions: is my tree protected, and if I am in a conservation area, what do I send to the council? A short video from the UK consultancy channel Ask The Arb answers the first. This guide adds the second, with Canterbury City Council's own links and the four conservation areas that touch Bridge.

Watch first: Is Your Tree Protected? (Ask The Arb)

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=lPoTE9uTrLI. Credit: the Ask The Arb channel, presented by the consultant who runs it. The demonstration uses another English council, so treat it as a walk-through of the method rather than of Canterbury's screens.

What the video shows

It is under four minutes and it is a screen-share of how to check a property for a conservation area and a Tree Preservation Order (TPO) using a council's online map. Roughly:

  • From about 1:00, search for your local authority, then search its website for “trees” and look for a protected trees page.
  • Around 1:45, most councils now have an online mapping tool: enter a postcode and pick the property.
  • Around 2:25, the property panel shows whether the address is in a conservation area.
  • From about 2:35, switch on the conservation area and TPO layers on the map, then zoom out to see how the boundaries sit around the property.
  • Near the end, click a TPO on the map to open its details.

That is steps one and two of the process below. What it does not cover is what Bridge looks like on that map, what counts as a tree, or anything about the notice itself.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the notice itself

Which council, and which conservation areas. Bridge is in the Canterbury district, and Canterbury City Council is the planning authority, as it is for the rest of this site's patch. Its conservation area layer lists four areas that touch Bridge: Bridge, Bifrons Park (shared with Bekesbourne with Patrixbourne), Bourne Park (shared with Bishopsbourne) and Renville Farm and Bridge Railway Station. Neighbouring villages have their own, including Patrixbourne, Bekesbourne, Bishopsbourne and Barham. The council says there are 97 in the district in total. Start with the council's conservation areas page and search your address on its Planning Constraints map, which the council also labels “Check if a tree is protected”. Do the check on the plot, not the road name: four areas touch the village, so an address near the edge of one can sit outside the boundary next door.

Which trees need notice?

The rule is section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990: notice to the council at least six weeks before cutting down, topping, lopping, uprooting, wilfully damaging or wilfully destroying a tree in a conservation area. The tests, from the government guidance:

  • Size: the trunk must be over 75mm across, measured over the bark at 1.5m above ground. At 75mm or less there is no notice for cutting down, topping, lopping or uprooting. There is a 100mm limit too, but only for cutting down or uprooting to help other trees grow (thinning), not for pruning.
  • How to measure: tape round the trunk at 1.5m, divide by 3.14. A field maple that measures 38cm round is 38 divided by 3.14, which is 12.1cm, so 121mm: well over. A hazel stem of 20cm round is 64mm: under. If the tree has several stems at 1.5m and any one is over the limit, the exception is lost.
  • Type: species does not matter. Field maple, ash, hornbeam, beech, holly, yew, leylandii and other conifers all count if they are trees over the limit. Native or non-native, evergreen or deciduous.
  • Hedge plants: shrubs are not normally treated as trees, so ordinary clipping of the hedge itself is not what the notice is for. The guidance does say trees in a hedge, or an old hedge that has grown into a line of trees of reasonable height, can be treated as trees. If a Nailbourne-valley boundary of hawthorn has been let go for twenty years and you plan to cut the thick stems, ask the council first.
  • Exceptions: dead or dangerous trees and branches (below), work needed to carry out a full planning permission, work to prevent a legal nuisance (the minimum necessary), work required by an Act of Parliament, and pruning of trees cultivated for fruit in line with good horticultural practice. Removing dead branches does not need approval, but the council asks for five days' warning if you can give it.

A worked example. A hedge along a garden off Bridge High Street: hawthorn, hazel and hornbeam, clipped every year, with two field maples that have grown up through it. The clipped hedge plants are ordinary hedge care. The field maples measure 38cm and 27cm round: 121mm and 86mm, both over the line. Any pruning of either needs a notice. Meanwhile, a hedge trimmer used on the hawthorn does not. The nesting-law timing still applies to both.

TPO first. If the tree has a TPO, the normal TPO rules apply and you need the council's consent, not a six-week notice. The Planning Portal says a TPO overrides the conservation area.

The application, step by step

  1. Check the address. As above, on the council's map, for a conservation area and for TPOs.
  2. Check the tree. Measure it. Note the species and whether it is in the hedge line or beside it.
  3. Decide the work, and photograph it. Canterbury's guidance notes (PDF) say “cut back”, “lop” or “trim” is too vague. Use crown thinning (a percentage of leaf area; the council says over 30% is likely to be refused), crown lifting (to a stated height) or crown reduction (to a stated height and spread).
  4. Gather the details. Species; a rough sketch plan with the tree numbered and any nearby trees you are not touching marked; the works and reasons, tree by tree; annotated photographs; your contact details; the date. The government guidance says a notice must carry its submission date.
  5. Send it. The council's route is Apply to do work to a tree in a conservation area on the Planning Portal. It has its own guidance note (PDF), and its works to trees page confirms there is no fee. The council's own work to trees page links to the same form.
  6. Wait six weeks. The council can make a TPO, tell you it will not and you can go ahead, or do nothing and let the six weeks end. It cannot refuse and cannot attach conditions, because a section 211 notice is not an application. If it makes a TPO, you may apply for consent under it.
  7. Afterwards. If no Order is made, do the work within two years of the date of the notice, exactly as described. Give your contractor the notice and plan, keep the confirmation, and check for nesting birds between 1 March and 31 August (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, section 1).
  8. Emergencies. A dead tree needs five days' notice to the council before the work. An immediate risk of serious harm allows action at once, with written notice as soon as practicable after. Dying or diseased is not dead. Photograph first, and expect a replacement tree to be required.

A short, precise entry works best, for example: “Tree 1 (field maple, 121mm at 1.5m, rear boundary hedge, marked 1 on the plan): crown reduction by about 1.5m, to an overall height of about 9m, to clear the neighbouring roofline. No work to tree 2. Photographs attached.”

Breaking section 211 is an offence: a fine of up to £20,000 in the magistrates' court, unlimited in the Crown Court, and a duty to replace any tree removed in breach.
