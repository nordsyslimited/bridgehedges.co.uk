Planting a bare-root hedge on North Downs chalk: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

The RHS is about as authoritative a source as UK gardening gets. Their "Grow at Home" bare-root planting video, filmed at RHS Garden Wisley by Trials Team Leader Alex Hankey, covers the whole process clearly. What it cannot cover is the particular conditions around Bridge: thin chalk over the North Downs, the wetter valley floor that the Nailbourne cuts through, and the species mix that establishes best on each. That is what this guide adds.

Watch first: How to plant bare root hedges – Grow at Home, Royal Horticultural Society

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=qDJc9rqCAY4

What the video shows

Presented from the Trials area at Wisley, the video works through the full planting sequence for a native mixed hedge using bare-root stock. It covers:

  • Why bare-root is the standard UK approach for new hedges: cheaper than pot-grown, planted while dormant (November through March), and with strong establishment rates when the soil is prepared correctly.
  • Plant spacing: roughly 3 plants per metre in a single row, or a double staggered row at closer spacing for a denser hedge from the start. The double-row approach uses more plants upfront but closes the gaps faster in the first few seasons.
  • The planting method: digging out a trench or individual holes deep enough to take the roots without bending them, spreading the roots, firming the plant in at the same depth as the nursery mark on the stem, and watering in well.
  • Weed control at planting: a mulch mat or a layer of bark around the base to suppress grass and weeds competing with the new plants in their first growing season.
  • The value of an initial hard cut at planting time: reducing the top growth by roughly half encourages bushy, multi-stem growth from low down, which is what makes a proper dense hedge rather than a row of single-stemmed whips.

The video is filmed at Wisley in Surrey, on the acidic sandy soils characteristic of that site. That difference from North Downs chalk is the main thing worth knowing before you apply the advice around Bridge.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Two different soils at Bridge. Bridge village sits at the head of the Nailbourne valley, roughly where the chalk of the North Downs meets the valley floor. The slopes above the village, running toward Barham, Kingston, and Lower Hardres, are chalk: free-draining, alkaline and sometimes thin over the underlying rock. The valley floor from Bridge down through Patrixbourne and Bekesbourne is heavier, wetter in winter when the Nailbourne (a winterbourne chalk stream that flows only in wetter years) is active, and prone to late-spring frost because cold air drains downhill and settles in the valley bottom. These are two noticeably different planting environments and they favour slightly different approaches.

Species for chalk slopes. Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is the single most reliable native hedging species on the North Downs, tolerating thin chalk and dry summers once established. Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) is almost as good, and it provides dense early thickening and excellent thorny stock-proofing. Field maple (Acer campestre) and wayfaring tree (Viburnum lantana) are both genuinely native to the North Downs chalk and make valuable additions to a mixed hedge. Hazel is fine where the chalk carries a reasonable depth of topsoil, but it can struggle to establish on very shallow, drought-stressed ground in its first summer, so it is worth watering in dry spells in years one and two. The hedge-laying guide on this site covers hawthorn and blackthorn in the context of traditional field-boundary work, which is the main use for both species on the chalk around Patrixbourne and Lower Hardres.

Species for the valley floor. Hawthorn and blackthorn perform equally well on the valley floor soils. The main additional consideration is waterlogging: if the planting line sits in a low spot that stands wet after rain, break up any compacted layer below the planting trench so excess water can drain rather than drowning the new root systems. A heeled-in bare-root plant sitting in standing water will not establish, whatever species it is.

Frost-pocket timing. The initial hard pruning the video recommends (the cut at or shortly after planting) is usually done in the dormant season, which keeps it well outside the core nesting period. If you are planting in late February or early March and plan to do the initial cut a few weeks later, check the hedge first. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use: the safe cutting window runs outside 1 March to 31 August. A newly planted hedge with dense cover is less of a nesting risk than an established one, but blackthorn in particular comes into leaf early and can attract early nesters. When in doubt, delay the cut to autumn rather than push it into spring. The nesting-law timing guide on this site covers the legal position in full.

Conservation-area removals before replanting. If you are replanting because an existing hedge was removed or died, and that hedge was inside or bordering the Bifrons Park Conservation Area, the removal work may have required a six-week notice to Canterbury City Council under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (stems of 75mm or more at 1.5m height, or trees over 75mm). New planting does not itself require notice. But it is worth checking the removal side before assuming the replanting alone covers the situation.

A note on the Wisley context. RHS Wisley sits on greensand, an acidic and free-draining but quite different soil from North Downs chalk. The planting technique in the video transfers directly, but any specific species recommendations within it should be weighed against what is known to do well on alkaline chalk soils rather than taken as universal. The native species list above is a safer guide for the Bridge area than any greensand-adapted suggestions.
