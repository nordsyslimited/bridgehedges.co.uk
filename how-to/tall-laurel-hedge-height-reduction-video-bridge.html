Trimming a tall cherry laurel hedge: a video companion for Bridge, Kent.

Many properties around Bridge have cherry laurel hedges that have grown well above a manageable working height. Burncoose Nurseries, whose laurel guides cover everything from the annual maintenance trim to full hard-pruning renovation, have a video specifically about working with a taller hedge than their other demonstrations. This guide takes their approach and adds the Bridge-specific timing, staged-reduction strategy and Nailbourne-valley context that change how the job sits when the hedge is here rather than in Cornwall.

Watch first: How to trim a laurel hedge, Burncoose Nurseries

Watch on YouTube: youtube.com/watch?v=qhDNXLmASuY (Burncoose Nurseries)

What the video shows

Burncoose Nurseries is a family-run Cornish nursery with a long history in plant propagation and trialling. Their short how-to guides are working demonstrations, not classroom theory. This video is explicitly their tall-laurel companion to their other laurel trimming guides, and tackles a hedge that is significantly higher than the ones in those earlier videos. It demonstrates:

  • How the core laurel trimming principle -- hand tools over powered reciprocating cutters -- scales to a bigger working height. The leaf-bisection problem does not go away because the hedge is taller: cutting through a large laurel leaf still leaves brown, desiccated cut edges that persist for weeks, so the case for secateurs and loppers rather than a machine remains the same regardless of scale. The video shows how to apply this on a hedge that takes more reach and more time than a standard boundary planting.
  • Technique for working at height on a tall hedge. The video addresses the practical reality of trimming a hedge that cannot be reached from ground level, including how to maintain consistent coverage and clean cuts when working from a step ladder or platform rather than standing on the ground.
  • How much to take off in one pass. Cherry laurel can carry a hard cut, but on a tall hedge that has been trimmed regularly the goal is usually to keep the existing shape while containing the size, rather than removing large amounts in one go. The video demonstrates where to cut to maintain a healthy, dense face rather than exposing bare wood.
  • What recovery looks like: laurel's reliable ability to push new growth from the cut surface, which is what makes it one of the more forgiving subjects for a working-at-height trim even when the job is physically demanding.

What it doesn't cover: Bridge and the Nailbourne valley

Burncoose's demonstration is produced in Cornwall on a specific hedge, so here is what changes when the hedge is in Bridge or the Nailbourne valley villages.

When trimming becomes staged reduction. There is a practical difference between trimming a tall hedge at its current height and actually bringing that height down. If the hedge has been allowed to reach 4 m or 5 m and needs to end up at 2 m, trimming at the current level every year simply maintains the problem. Staged reduction means taking off a meaningful amount of height each autumn -- say 30 to 40 cm -- and committing to repeat the process over three or four years, so the hedge retreats without the drastic leaf-stripping that would come from taking the whole height off in one pass. This is the approach I use on taller hedges around Bridge that need to come down without going bare: the Burncoose technique for clean individual cuts applies at every stage of the process, it just gets repeated more aggressively each year. For a hedge that has been severely neglected and needs to come down quickly, the hard-pruning guide covers the more radical approach.

Nesting-season timing. The timing constraint that matters most on the lanes around Bridge is legal rather than horticultural. Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while that nest is in use or being built. Cherry laurel hedges -- dense, dark and impenetrable from outside -- are among the most heavily used nesting habitats in domestic gardens in this part of Kent. The nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August as a general guide. For a tall hedge that needs significant cutting, September is the safest month: the nesting season has closed, the main growth flush of the season is largely done, and the cut ends have several weeks of mild weather to close before the first frosts. I always check the full canopy for active nests immediately before starting any cut between March and August, and I'll stop if I find occupied ones.

Growth rate on valley-floor ground. Cherry laurel grows noticeably faster on the alluvial and clay-with-flints soils of the Nailbourne valley bottom than on the chalk slopes above Bridge village. A hedge that puts on 30 to 40 cm of new growth per season on the chalk can put on 50 cm or more in the valley bottom, particularly in a wet year. For a tall hedge on a valley-floor site, this means the problem compounds faster: a hedge left for two seasons in Bekesbourne or Patrixbourne will have added more height than the same hedge above Bridge would have. This is not a reason to avoid working on these hedges, but it is worth factoring into how often the work needs to happen.

Working at height: when to call a professional. Trimming a hedge of 2 to 2.5 m with a step ladder and long-handled secateurs is a reasonable DIY job for most people. At 3 m and above, the combination of a powered or long-reach cutting tool, an unstable surface, and falling material from above becomes a genuinely different risk calculation. For hedges in the 3 m to 5 m range I use a podium platform rather than a ladder for the working face, and I would not carry out cutting at that height alone. For a hedge taller than about 5 m, scaffold access is usually needed to do the job without working from a leaning ladder with the tool extended above your head. The annual maintenance trim guide covers tool choice and working practice for the more typical domestic height; this guide and the Burncoose video address the step above that.

Leaf toxicity and arisings. Cherry laurel produces large volumes of arisings when the hedge is tall. The leaves and berries contain compounds that break down to produce prussic acid, and the clippings are toxic to horses and livestock. Around the farms and smallholdings on the lanes towards Lower Hardres, Bishopsbourne and Kingston, I bag all laurel clippings and take them off site rather than leaving them on the ground or adding them to a compost pile. It is worth confirming the disposal approach with the property owner before starting, particularly on agricultural properties where cuttings might be accessible to animals.

Conservation-area check. A tall, established cherry laurel hedge inside the Bifrons Park Conservation Area can have main stems that exceed the 75 mm diameter threshold at 1.5 m height, which triggers the six-week notice requirement under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 for work on trees and hedges. Routine trimming of established topgrowth generally does not require notice, but significant height reduction on a large hedge inside the CA is worth confirming with Canterbury City Council before starting. The conservation area notice guide covers the process in full.
