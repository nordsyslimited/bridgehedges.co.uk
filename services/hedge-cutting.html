Service · Hedge cutting

Routine cuts done properly, on every species I work with.

The bread-and-butter job: annual or twice-yearly cuts on hedges already at a good height. Done right, this is what keeps a hedge looking like it belongs to the house.

What's included

  • Top, sides and front face cut to your chosen height and width
  • All arisings cleared from site and disposed of properly (Canterbury's Vauxhall Road recycling centre for garden waste; chipped for reuse where it makes sense)
  • Brush-down of the path, lawn or driveway underneath
  • Six-week section-211 notice filed on your behalf if you're inside the Bifrons Park Conservation Area and the hedge has any stems over 75mm at chest height
  • Fixed price quoted in advance, no day-rate creep

Timing by species: chalk versus valley floor

Bridge sits where the North Downs chalk drops into the Nailbourne valley, and that split genuinely changes the cutting calendar. Hedges on the open downland above the village dry out and finish their growth flush earlier than hedges down by the stream, where the ground holds moisture and cold air pools in a frost pocket through winter and into spring.

  • Hawthorn, blackthorn: the reliable native field-boundary pair on Bridge's chalk topsoil. Winter only (September to February) to comply with nesting law.
  • Hornbeam, beech: the North Downs' classic formal-hedge pair. Single annual cut, August or September, so the hedge holds its dead leaves through winter.
  • Yew: single late-summer or early-autumn cut; happy with a hard clip once a year and resentful of more. Genuinely native to this chalk and close to a no-compromise choice here.
  • Privet, box, Leylandii, laurel: fast-growing, twice a year (late spring and late summer) if inland gardens want a tighter formal line. Box needs an eye kept on it for box blight and box tree moth.
  • Field maple, holly: late summer to early autumn; both tolerate the valley-bottom frost pocket better than tender evergreens do.

Nesting-law timing

Section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with the core nesting season running 1 March to 31 August, extending into September for late broods. I check for active nests before any work booked close to that window, whether it's a garden hedge in the village or a field boundary out towards Patrixbourne. This is written into every quote where it applies.

What I don't do on cutting jobs

If a hedge needs more than a routine cut: it's overgrown, gappy, leaning, or has outgrown the position, that's a reduction job, not a cut. I tell you that at quote stage rather than do a botched cut and pretend it solved the problem.
